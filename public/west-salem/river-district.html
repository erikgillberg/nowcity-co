A Proposal for Study

How might Salem become the most livable city in America by 2050?

A river district on both banks, the case for building it, and the work it would take to find out whether any of this holds.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Ambition

Thirty thousand more people, and the ones already here who leave

Salem is a city of about 183,000 that has been growing slowly and quietly for a long time. Suppose it set out to add thirty thousand people over the next twenty years, deliberately rather than by drift. The number itself is not the hard part. It is roughly the trend line. The hard part is who those people are, whether the city gives its own young people a reason to stay, and whether the places they would live are worth living in.

A city does not become livable by accident, and it does not become livable quickly. It becomes livable because a generation of people decided it should and then held the line for thirty years.

The Question

Why this is a fair question rather than a boast

We looked at the bigger version of this question first and published the answer we did not want. The global livability rankings turn on things a city does not control: national health systems, gun laws, social insurance, the cost of higher education. Salem cannot win a contest decided in Washington. So the world question is the wrong one to ask.

The American question is different

Within the United States, every city plays by the same national rules. What separates the best American places from the rest is almost entirely local: land use, walkable density, a public realm worth walking through, a working waterfront, housing that ordinary earners can afford, tree canopy, and the patience to hold a plan for decades.

Which makes it answerable

Those levers belong to a city, its region and the people who build in it. That is why the question is worth asking out loud in Salem, and why it is a question rather than a claim. We do not get to decide the answer. We would like to be useful to the people who do.

The Starting Position

What Salem already has

Most cities chasing this would have to invent their advantages. Salem's are already in place and mostly under-used.

A river through the middle

The Willamette runs between downtown and West Salem with public land, an island park and two bridges. In most American cities this would be the most valuable ground for miles. Here it is largely industrial, fenced or forgotten.

A capital city's institutions

The state government, a hospital system, two universities within reach, community colleges running the exact trades an advanced-manufacturing economy needs. Stable employment through cycles that flatten other mid-size cities.

A position on the corridor

Salem sits within an hour of Portland, the Hillsboro semiconductor cluster, Oregon State and Eugene. It is the only point on that line within reach of all of them, and the only one that is also the seat of government.

Industry that is already here

Food processing, wood products, logistics, advanced manufacturing and a humanoid robotics factory. The jobs base is more interesting than Salem's reputation suggests, and it spans several wage levels rather than one.

Room, and relative affordability

Land inside the urban growth boundary, industrial buildings worth adapting, and prices that still let a household on local wages consider ownership. That combination has almost vanished on the West Coast.

An adopted plan that says so

Salem's comprehensive plan already calls for housing near major employment centers, mixed-use riverfront development and support for emerging industries. The policy basis for this work exists. What is missing is the demonstration.

The Gap

And what it is missing

Being honest about this is the whole basis for doing anything. Salem is not one decision away from being Portland, and it should not want to be.

01
Walkable density anywhere outside a few downtown blocks

Most of the city is built at a scale that requires a car for everything, which sets a ceiling on street life before anyone has tried.

02
A riverfront doing anything useful

The asset that would define the city is mostly turned away from. There is no continuous public edge, and almost nowhere to eat, work, live or launch a boat from.

03
Housing supply at the pace the state now requires

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions. The strategy is real. The delivery capacity to meet it is the open question.

04
A reason for a young person to stay

Salem educates people and then watches a share of them leave for Portland or further. They leave for work, for rent they can justify, and for somewhere to be on a Saturday night.

05
A reason to cross the river

West Salem is a bedroom. Downtown empties in the evening. Neither side gives the other a destination, and the bridges carry commuters rather than life.

The Proposition

One river district, on both banks

The original plan was a neighborhood in West Salem. That was too small a question. The river is the thing Salem has and does not use, and it has two sides. Treating both banks and the water between them as one district changes what is possible: a continuous public edge, a crossing that carries people rather than cars, and enough combined scale to justify the infrastructure that makes a place work.

The Innovation District

West Salem's industrial blocks and the downtown waterfront, held together by the river rather than divided by it. Workspace, homes, a market, and the public edge that neither side has today.

The Extension

A long stretch of west-bank waterfront land upstream, most of it undeveloped, which is where a district of this ambition finds the room to keep going once the first phases prove out.

The Masterplan

What would actually be built

A working framework rather than a proposal. Every piece of it is a hypothesis about what a place needs, and every piece is meant to be argued with.

01
Edgewater East and West

Mixed-use innovation and residential districts on either bank, so the district has a daytime population and an evening one.

02
The Greenway and the Riverfront Promenade

A continuous central park and a public trail and habitat edge along the water, which is the piece that makes everything else worth being near.

03
Community Commons

Sports fields, a market, a civic lawn and a place for the events a city needs a home for.

04
The Maker's Live-Work Hub

Light industrial space and workshops with homes above, in buildings that already exist, which is the cheapest and fastest thing to start with.

05
Downtown Urban Core

Retail, dining and civic frontage on the east bank, so the district strengthens downtown instead of competing with it.

06
A mobility hub and a pedestrian bridge

Transit, parking held in one place rather than spread everywhere, and a crossing built for people, so both banks become one walkable place.

The Extension

Where it goes next

A large piece of west-bank waterfront gives the district somewhere to grow into once the first phases have proved the demand. It is also the part that could carry a genuine landmark, and the reason to think in decades rather than phases.

The Point of It

What a Tuesday would feel like

Plans are judged on drawings. Places are judged on ordinary days. These are studies of West Salem's existing industrial buildings adapted, and of the life a district like this is designed to hold.

The Economy

Jobs at several wage levels, in buildings that already stand

A district that only produces high-wage technology jobs would miss most of Salem. The work this place could hold runs from timber fabrication and food production through maker space and light industry to research and engineering. That range is the point, and it is also what makes the housing question answerable, because a district has to house the people who work in it.

Make things here

Oregon has a mass-timber sector, a federal Tech Hub for it, and a wood products industry already in the valley. Building components are the most obvious thing for this district to produce, and the housing shortage is the customer.

Keep the young ones

Community college and university programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics already exist here. What is missing is somewhere for a graduate to work and somewhere they can afford to live within a short walk of it.

House the workforce

The employment growth already happening on the east side of the city produces demand for homes that the city has not yet built. This district is the closest available answer to that, on both banks.

What We Do Not Know

Three questions that have to be answered by someone other than us

Everything above is an argument, and arguments are cheap. None of it should be believed until it has been studied by people with no stake in the answer. Three pieces of work would test it, and each is worth funding whichever way it comes out.

01 · Market and workforce

Who lives where now, how much of the advanced-industry workforce commutes in, what would move them, and what housing types and rents the corridor actually supports across income bands. Including how many jobs, built from a published method rather than asserted.

02 · Site and district feasibility

Both banks: title, environmental, geotechnical, utilities, stormwater, transportation and the entitlement pathway. Findings sorted plainly into no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or potential fatal flaw.

03 · The manufacturing question

Whether a building-components manufacturing base serving the region holds up: aggregated demand, transport economics, the state code pathway, financing that fits factory production, and honest cost against conventional construction.

If the argument holds, this is the basis for a district and a great deal of housing. If it fails, Salem learns cheaply and precisely why, and so does every other mid-size city working on the same problem. That is why the work is worth public support either way, and why the findings should be published in full rather than held privately.

The Ask

Fund the questions, not the vision

We are looking for a public or institutional sponsor to host and fund the feasibility work.

Now City is not an eligible applicant for most of the funding that exists for work like this, and that is appropriate. Public money should not pay a private developer to study its own site. What we are asking for is a partner who can hold the work, commission it independently, and publish it: a city, a county, an economic development organization, a university or a foundation.

What that produces is a public asset. An independent, published answer to whether a river district in Salem can carry housing, jobs and a waterfront worth walking. Every developer, employer and public body that comes after gets to use it, whatever they conclude about us.

It is a question, not a claim. Salem gets to answer it.
