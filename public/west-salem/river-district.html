A Proposal for Study

How might Salem become the most livable city in America by 2050?

A river district on both banks of the Willamette, and the studies that would test whether it works.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Ambition

Adding thirty thousand people over twenty years

Salem has about 183,000 residents and has been growing at well under one percent a year. Adding thirty thousand people over twenty years is close to that trend line, so the number is achievable. What matters more is the composition: whether the city keeps the young people it educates, whether homes and jobs arrive together, and where the homes go.

The Question

Is there a way to add thirty thousand people while traffic falls and daily life and local ecology improve?

Growth in most American cities produces the opposite: more cars, longer trips, more farmland consumed, more runoff into the river. That outcome follows from where the homes are put rather than from the number of people.

Traffic

Households in walkable, mixed-use areas make fewer and shorter vehicle trips than households at the edge of a city. Homes placed near work, schools, shops and transit reduce total driving even as the population rises.

Daily life

Proximity is what gives people time back. A shorter commute, a school within walking distance and a market on the way home change how a day is spent more than any single amenity does.

Local ecology

Reusing buildings that already stand consumes no new land, and compact development leaves farmland and habitat intact. At district scale, stormwater can be cleaned before it reaches the river, the bank can be replanted, and canopy and habitat can be added rather than removed.

This is the question the district is designed to test. We do not know the answer, and the studies described at the end of this document are how it would be measured rather than assumed.

The Comparison

Livability in America is decided locally

We examined the global version of this question first and published what we found. International livability rankings turn on national policy: health systems, gun laws, social insurance, the cost of higher education. A single American city has no influence over any of them.

The American comparison works differently

Every city in the United States operates under the same national rules. What separates the best American places from the rest is local: land use, walkable density, the quality of the public realm, a usable waterfront, housing that ordinary earners can afford, tree canopy, and holding a plan steady for decades.

Which makes the question answerable

Those levers belong to the city, the region and the people who build in them. Salem decides the answer. We are putting the question in front of the people who would have to work on it, along with what we have found so far.

The Starting Position

What Salem already has

These conditions are in place today and are mostly under-used.

A river through the middle

The Willamette runs between downtown and West Salem, with public land, an island park and two bridges. Most of the frontage is currently industrial or fenced.

Capital city institutions

State government, a regional hospital system, universities within reach, and community colleges running programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics. Employment here is steadier through downturns than in comparable cities.

A position on the corridor

Salem is within an hour of Portland, the Hillsboro semiconductor cluster, Oregon State and Eugene. It is the only point on that line within reach of all four, and the seat of state government.

An existing industrial base

Food processing, wood products, logistics, advanced manufacturing and a humanoid robotics factory. The jobs span several wage levels.

Land and relative affordability

Developable land inside the urban growth boundary, industrial buildings suitable for adaptation, and prices that still allow ownership on local wages.

Supportive adopted policy

Salem's comprehensive plan calls for housing near major employment centers, mixed-use riverfront development and support for emerging industries. The policy basis exists.

The Gaps

What is missing today

Five conditions stand between Salem and the question on the cover. Each is addressable, and each takes longer than one project.

01
Walkable density outside a few downtown blocks

Most of the city is built at a scale that requires a car for every trip, which limits street life and raises household transport costs.

02
A working riverfront

There is no continuous public edge along the water, and few places to eat, work, live or launch a boat.

03
Housing delivery at the pace the state now requires

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions. The open question is delivery capacity.

04
Reasons for young people to stay

Salem educates people who then move to Portland or further for work, for rents they can justify and for a wider social life.

05
A destination on each side of the river

West Salem is largely residential and downtown empties in the evening, so the bridges carry commuters in the morning and again at five.

The Proposition

One river district on both banks

The original plan covered a neighborhood in West Salem. The area under study is now both sides of the river. Treating both banks and the water between them as a single district allows a continuous public edge, a crossing built for people, and enough combined scale to justify shared infrastructure.

The Innovation District

West Salem's industrial blocks and the downtown waterfront, connected across the river. Workspace, homes, a market and a public edge on both sides.

The Extension

A large area of west-bank land downstream, most of it undeveloped, which gives the district room to continue once the first phases are complete.

The Economy

The work the district would hold

The employment here would span several wage levels. Fabrication, food production, maker space and light industry, research and engineering. That range matters, because a district has to house the people who work in it.

Large-format industrial development belongs at Mill Creek Corporate Center on the east side of the city, which is already built and marketed for it. This district holds the smaller-format work that fits beside housing: workshops, assembly, studios and labs in buildings that already stand.

Make building components

Oregon has a mass-timber sector, a federal Tech Hub for it, and an established wood products industry in the valley. The regional housing shortage is the demand.

Retain graduates

Community college and university programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics already operate here. What is required is local work and housing within reach of it.

House the workforce

Employment growth on the east side of the city creates housing demand the city has not yet met. This district is within reach of it on both banks.

What We Do Not Know

Three studies

Everything above is an argument. It should be tested by people with no stake in the result. Three pieces of work would do that, and each is worth funding whichever way it comes out.

01 · Market and workforce

Who lives where now, how much of the advanced-industry workforce commutes in, what would move them, and what housing types and rents the corridor supports across income bands. Including a jobs estimate built from a published method.

02 · Site and district feasibility

Both banks: title, environmental, geotechnical, utilities, stormwater, transportation and the entitlement pathway. Findings sorted into no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or potential fatal flaw.

03 · Manufacturing feasibility

Whether a building-components manufacturing base serving the region holds up: aggregated demand, transport economics, the state code pathway, financing that fits factory production, and cost against conventional construction.

The question about traffic, daily life and local ecology should be measured within this scope: vehicle trips per household against Salem averages, land consumed per home, and the difference between adapting a building and replacing it. If the argument holds, this is the basis for a district and a substantial amount of housing. If it fails, Salem learns why at low cost, and so does every other mid-size city working on the same problem. The findings should be published in full either way.

The Ask

What we are asking for

Public and institutional sponsors to hold and fund the feasibility work.

Now City is not an eligible applicant for most of the funding available for work of this kind, and that is appropriate: public money should not pay a private developer to study its own site. What is needed are partners who can hold the work, commission it independently and publish it: cities, counties, economic development organizations, universities and foundations. The three studies can be split between several sponsors, which is often easier than finding one to carry all of it.

The result is a public asset. An independent, published answer to whether a river district in Salem can carry housing, jobs and a usable waterfront, and whether it can do so while traffic and environmental impact fall. Every developer, employer and public body that follows can use those findings.
