What's good for the capital of Oregon is good for all of Oregon. A standing civic coalition for Salem's riverfront generation.

01 · The Premise

Capital cities underperform. The ones that don't chose to organize.

American state capitals routinely trail their states' largest cities: in investment, in identity, in the ambition applied to their own ground. Salem and Portland. Olympia and Seattle. Springfield and Chicago. It is a pattern so consistent it feels like a law of nature.

It isn't. Every capital that broke the pattern broke it the same way: not with a single project, but with a standing coalition. Business, civic, institutional, and public leadership at one table, organized around one legible thesis, with a vehicle that could actually deliver. Indianapolis did it. Columbus did it. Oklahoma City, Des Moines, Chattanooga did it on their rivers.

Salem has the tables: its chambers, its collaboratives, its agencies, its philanthropies. What it has never had is the shared vehicle that turns those tables toward the city's single greatest underused asset: a working riverfront in the heart of Oregon's capital.

03 · The Mechanism

Five features every version shares

A named, permanent vehicle

Not an ad-hoc alliance around one deal. An entity with a charter, co-owned by its members, that outlasts any single project and any single leader.

A demonstration project in year one

Coalitions that begin with a plan produce plans. Coalitions that begin with a visible project produce belief. The Edgewater District riverfront is Salem's.

Principals only, with skin in

The people at the table are the people who can commit their organizations: landowners, builders, institutional leaders, public officials, each contributing dues, capacity, or land to the shared work.

A small professional staff

Volunteer energy decays. A lean secretariat keeps the work moving between convenings and carries institutional memory across years.

One legible thesis

Ours is on this page. Salem is the last affordable riverfront capital on the West Coast, and the strength of Oregon's capital city is a statewide interest: economically, civically, and symbolically.

04 · Year One

The Edgewater District as demonstration project

The Compact's first work is already underway: the transformation of a recently up-zoned industrial corridor on the Willamette River, minutes from the Capitol and from Willamette University, into a walkable riverfront district. Housing people can afford, market halls, innovation space, and public access to a river Salem has worked beside for a century but rarely been able to touch.

The district is planned the Now City way: land and natural systems first, so that what gets built is what the place can carry. The Compact's role is civic, and its members will shape it together. The early work is simple: bring builders, landowners, institutions, and public partners into one working relationship, and tell Salem's story to the rest of Oregon. The rest emerges the way it did in every precedent city: from the table itself.

05 · The Table

Seats at the table

District builders

Developers and operators who have delivered walkable, mixed-use places in the Northwest and want a hand in the capital's next chapter.

Landowners

Riverfront and corridor owners whose ground is the district, and whose participation shapes what it becomes.

Civic & philanthropic institutions

Foundations, chambers, and community organizations already convening Salem's tables on education, health, and opportunity.

Education & research anchors

Universities, colleges, and training institutions whose students and programs give a district its daytime life.

Public partners

City, county, regional, and state agencies whose tools, from urban renewal to infrastructure to economic development, make district work possible.

Founding participants

Announced as they join. The Compact convenes its first table in 2026.
