Concept · Regional Mobility

The tracks are
already here.

A working freight line runs through the district. What would it take to give that corridor a second job, and connect West Salem to the valley and eventually to Portland without building a new right of way?

A development and mobility hypothesis · Not an approved project · Requires railroad, regulatory and technical validation
The Asset

The hardest part of a regional transit system is the part Salem already has

Corridors are what make transit expensive and slow: acquiring right of way, clearing environmental review, and building civil works through a city that is already built. Salem is not starting there. A rail corridor runs along Front Street through the district, and it has run through this valley for more than a century.

There is history worth knowing here. On New Year's Day 1908, the Oregon Electric Railway began carrying passengers between Salem and Portland, and it kept doing so until 1933. Electrified freight continued on those lines until 1945. The idea that this valley's rail could move people between its cities is not speculative. It is a function the corridor once performed, on electric power, and then stopped performing.

Today the corridor carries relatively low-volume freight. That is a real economic function and it deserves protection. It is also the thing that makes the corridor interesting: lightly used rail is exactly where the current generation of battery passenger technology is being deployed, because the track is already there and the capacity is not fully spoken for.

Build a new corridor

  • Right of way acquisition
  • Years of environmental review
  • Major civil construction
  • Very high capital requirement
  • Politically difficult, in public, for years
VERSUS

Give an existing one another job

  • Right of way exists
  • Track, bridges and crossings exist
  • Regional connectivity exists
  • Already in transportation use
  • Potential for incremental modernization
To be clear about what this is not: no passenger operating rights exist, no railroad has agreed to anything, and the corridor's current condition and capacity are unknown to us. The point of this page is that the question is worth asking, and that asking it properly is cheap compared to what it would cost to be wrong about the alternative.
The Geography

What a corridor would change

Transportation changes land value because it changes what is reachable. A functioning regional connection would begin to move Edgewater out of a purely Salem frame of reference and into the economic geography of the valley and the metro beyond it.

Today

West Salem's connection to the rest of the city runs through a small number of bridges. Most trips require a car. The regional connection is I-5, and it behaves like every other American interstate at rush hour.

Potentially

A rail connection that treats West Salem as a starting point rather than a suburb, with the district's mobility hub as the transfer point between regional travel and local movement.

The Stack

Regional speed. Local freedom.

This corridor concept is not a replacement for the mobility plan already published on this site. It is the layer above it. The four layers described in Salem in Motion handle movement inside the district and the city. Rail handles the distances those layers cannot.

The systems make each other better, which is the actual argument. Regional rail is only as useful as what you can do when you step off it, so a Whoosh circulator raises the value of every arriving train. The circulator is only as useful as the density around its stops, so development raises its value. And a district built around both needs less parking, which changes what can be built at all.

The goal is not to replace a car with a different vehicle you have to own. It is a network dense enough that most trips stop requiring one.

The Technology

Three generations, and none of them require inventing anything

This concept does not depend on a single speculative technology arriving on schedule. It depends on a corridor, which already exists, and vehicles, which are improving quickly. Each generation below is real work by real companies, at very different stages of maturity, and we label them honestly.

Generation one

Battery passenger rail on existing track

Remanufactured battery trainsets running on lightly used freight lines, with simple stations and minimal new infrastructure. Pop-Up Metro, a Railroad Development Corporation company now merged with the UK's TDI Greenway, has built its proposition on exactly this: let a community prove demand before committing to conventional commuter rail. Two battery trainsets are in demonstration operation on a test track in Pennsylvania.

Demonstration ready
Generation two

Very light rail

Smaller, lighter vehicles that cut both capital and operating cost, allowing higher frequency and much smaller stations. Coventry's Very Light Rail programme in the UK completed a demonstrator section in 2025 and is testing toward passenger service, targeting roughly half the cost and half the delivery time of conventional light rail, with a shallow track form that avoids most utility relocation.

In testing
Generation three

Autonomous battery vehicles

Small autonomous electric rail vehicles running individually or in platoons rather than as trains. Parallel Systems received Federal Railroad Administration approval and began a commercial pilot on 160 miles of Georgia railroad, and has tested compatibility with existing train control alongside a Class I railroad. Their focus today is freight, not passengers. The relevance here is what it proves about operating autonomous battery vehicles on ordinary American track.

Freight pilot underway
One connection worth noting, because it is a matter of public record rather than a claim: Parallel Systems' pilot partner is Genesee & Wyoming, and Portland & Western, the regional railroad that operates in this valley and keeps its executive offices in Salem, is a Genesee & Wyoming company. That is not an introduction, an agreement, or a plan. It is a reason to think the conversation is worth having with people who are already in the state.

Further out, research programmes in Europe including Urbanloop, TaxiRail and Pods4Rail are testing on-demand, individually routed rail concepts. We list them as research, not as options, and we would not plan around them.

The Constraint

Passengers and freight, not passengers instead of freight

The most common way a concept like this fails is by treating the railroad's existing business as an obstacle. The corridor performs an economic function today, its customers depend on it, and no serious proposal begins by proposing to take it away.

The relevant idea is temporal separation, and it is the same idea Pop-Up Metro built its model on: freight moves at night and in defined windows, passengers move during the day, and the corridor does two jobs on one set of rails. Whether that works on this corridor depends on things we do not yet know, including freight volumes and timing, track condition, signaling, passing capacity, and the railroad's own appetite.

Operating strategies worth studying, in roughly the order they would be studied: scheduled freight and passenger windows, signaling modernization, dispatch integration, selective passing infrastructure or double tracking, freight consolidation, and eventually battery or autonomous freight operations that change the economics for the railroad as well.

Stated plainly: a shared corridor has to be worth more to the railroad than an exclusive one. If it is not, the concept fails at the first meeting, and it should.

The Value

Mobility creates place value

Accessibility is the mechanism. When more of a region is reachable without a car, the ground under a district behaves differently, and the effects compound in a loop.

Transit accessibility has historically created the conditions for a larger effective labor market, lower household transportation costs, reduced parking requirements, greater development capacity, stronger retail foot traffic, larger visitor catchments and stronger station-area economics. Those conditions tend to show up in land value.

We are deliberately not putting a number on that here. Any figure produced before a feasibility study would be invented, and this is a hypothesis to be modeled during predevelopment rather than a projected outcome. What the flywheel does justify is studying the corridor early, because if it works, it changes the underwriting of everything built around it.

Funding mechanisms worth studying

Tax increment and urban renewal, station area and joint development, development agreements, infrastructure districts, public-private partnerships, land value capture, federal and state transportation funding, clean mobility and demonstration grants, and technology partnerships. None of these is committed, applied for, or approved. They are the menu a feasibility study would price.

The Path

Start small. Prove it. Expand.

The fastest way to discredit this idea would be to open with Portland. The credible sequence starts with questions, then a short demonstration, and earns the right to each following step.

Phase 0

Corridor discovery

Ownership, operating rights, freight customers and volumes, track and bridge condition, crossings, signaling, right of way width, station opportunities, regulatory jurisdiction, and connections to existing transit. Mostly desk work and conversations. Cheap, and it either opens the door or closes it honestly.

Phase 1

A Salem demonstration

A short segment that tests vehicle technology, operating compatibility with freight, station design, charging, community response and actual ridership. Edgewater is the natural hub for it, because the district is being built anyway and can host the stop, the charging and the crowd.

Phase 2

A Salem and Keizer spine

Extend to a useful passenger catchment and integrate properly with Cherriots, cycling infrastructure and the district circulator, so the rail line is part of a network rather than a novelty.

Phase 3

Regional connection

Explore extension north through the valley and toward the Portland metro, and connections into the regional transportation network. This is the phase everyone wants to start with, and the one that only works if the earlier phases have proven demand.

Phase 4

An autonomous corridor

As regulation and technology mature, smaller vehicles at higher frequency with increasing automation. The corridor does not need to be rebuilt for this. The vehicles change; the infrastructure stays.

The destination is not a train. It is a transportation protocol running on rail infrastructure, where the vehicles can evolve without rebuilding the corridor.

Open Questions

What we do not know yet

This page would be worth less if it pretended otherwise. These are the questions Phase 0 exists to answer, and any one of them could end the idea.

Who holds the rightsOwnership of the line, operating rights, and what a passenger operator would actually need to negotiate.
What the freight business needsCurrent customers, volumes, timing, and what a shared corridor would have to be worth to the railroad.
What the track can takeCondition, bridges, crossings, signaling, and the cost of bringing any of it to passenger standard.
Who regulates itFederal, state and local jurisdiction, and what approvals a demonstration would actually require.
Whether anyone would rideHonest demand estimates for the segments that could realistically run first.
What it costsCapital and operating cost for a demonstration, and who would fund and operate it.

Partners who would need to be part of answering these: the City of Salem, ODOT, SKATS and the regional council, Cherriots, Portland & Western, Oregon State University and other institutions, mobility technology companies, and the landowners and employers along the corridor. We are one participant among many, and on most of these questions we are not the ones with the answers.

The Larger Idea

A nineteenth-century corridor, a twenty-first-century system

For a century, railroads decided where American cities grew. Then we stopped building around them, and in most places the corridors quietly kept running with a fraction of the traffic they were built for. The infrastructure is still there. Battery propulsion, lighter vehicles, automation and better networks now make it reasonable to ask a question that would have sounded naive twenty years ago.

What if the railroad became city-building infrastructure again? Salem has the corridor, a district being planned around it, and a regional railroad headquartered in town. That is an unusually good place to find out.

Mobility companies

A district willing to host a demonstration, with a development team that can build the stop, the charging and the ridership around it.

Public agencies

A privately funded first study of a corridor question the region will eventually have to answer anyway.

Railroads and investors

A conversation about whether a shared corridor is worth more than an exclusive one, and what it would take to find out.
