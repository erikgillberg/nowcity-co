Concept · Regional Mobility

The tracks are
already here.

Oregon connected this valley with electric passenger trains a century ago, and much of the geography survived as freight. What would it take to make that infrastructure useful again?

A development and mobility hypothesis · Not an approved project · Requires railroad, regulatory and technical validation
The Asset

The hardest part of a regional transit system is the part Salem already has

Corridors are what make transit expensive and slow: acquiring right of way, clearing environmental review, and building civil works through a city that is already built. Salem is not starting there. A rail corridor runs along Front Street through the district, and it has run through this valley for more than a century.

There is history worth knowing here. On New Year's Day 1908, the Oregon Electric Railway began carrying passengers between Salem and Portland, and it kept doing so until 1933. Electrified freight continued on those lines until 1945. The idea that this valley's rail could move people between its cities is not speculative. It is a function the corridor once performed, on electric power, and then stopped performing.

Today the corridor carries relatively low-volume freight. That is a real economic function and it deserves protection. It is also the thing that makes the corridor interesting: lightly used rail is exactly where the current generation of battery passenger technology is being deployed, because the track is already there and the capacity is not fully spoken for.

Build a new corridor

  • Right of way acquisition
  • Years of environmental review
  • Major civil construction
  • Very high capital requirement
  • Politically difficult, in public, for years
VERSUS

Give an existing one another job

  • Right of way exists
  • Track, bridges and crossings exist
  • Regional connectivity exists
  • Already in transportation use
  • Potential for incremental modernization
To be clear about what this is not: no passenger operating rights exist, no railroad has agreed to anything, and the corridor's current condition and capacity are unknown to us. The point of this page is that the question is worth asking, and that asking it properly is cheap compared to what it would cost to be wrong about the alternative.
The Precedent

Oregon already built an electric valley once

This is the part of the story that surprises people. A connected, electric Willamette Valley is not a futuristic concept borrowed from Europe. It is something Oregon financed, built and operated, and then gave up.

1913
Electric

By 1913 the Oregon Electric Railway connected Portland, Salem, Albany and Eugene, with a branch to Corvallis. Electric passenger trains, running on a 122-mile valley mainline.

Today
Latent

The passengers left in 1933. Much of the transportation geography did not: corridors kept working as freight, and Amtrak Cascades still links the same valley cities a few times a day.

Next?
Layered

Same geography, different technology. Battery propulsion, lighter vehicles and automation raise a question that would have sounded naive twenty years ago.

Schematic only. Historic Oregon Electric alignments and today's active corridors are not identical, segments have been abandoned or repurposed, and nothing here should be read as an available route. Verifying what actually survives, and who controls it, is the first task on the list below.

1908

On New Year's Day, electric passenger trains begin running between Portland and Salem.

1912

The line reaches Albany on the Fourth of July, and Eugene by that October.

1913

A branch to Corvallis opens in March, connecting what are now Oregon's two largest public universities to the same electric network.

1933

Passenger service ends. The automobile, the highway and the motor bus changed the economics, as they did across America. Electrified freight continues until 1945.

Today

Freight still runs on much of the geography. Amtrak Cascades carries passengers through the same valley cities, sponsored by ODOT, at intercity frequencies rather than regional ones.

Next

A new generation of battery, lightweight and autonomous vehicles changes the equation again. The question is no longer whether Oregon can support regional electric mobility. It did, for twenty-five years. The question is what it could become now.

What Has Been Tried

This question is not new, and the record is useful

Oregon has spent three decades studying, and in one case building, passenger service on exactly these corridors. Anyone proposing something here should know that record cold, because it contains both the encouragement and the warnings.

2009 to today · Built

WES Commuter Rail

Oregon's first commuter rail: 14.7 miles from Beaverton to Wilsonville, owned by TriMet and operated by Portland & Western, running on a shared freight corridor. It exists, it works mechanically, and it has never come close to its ridership projections. Reporting has put the operating subsidy at roughly a hundred dollars per passenger.

2012 to 2021 · Studied

Oregon Passenger Rail

A federally funded Tier 1 environmental study of passenger rail between Eugene and Portland, concluded with a Record of Decision in April 2021. It examined building a new alignment along I-5 and rejected it, selecting instead the existing Amtrak Cascades route with incremental track, signal and communications work, noting that investment could be broken into relatively small, lower-cost pieces.

2019, 2024 · Proposed

WES extension to Salem and Keizer

Legislators and transit advocates have repeatedly pushed for a state study of extending WES south through Donald, Woodburn, Keizer and Salem, with a task force including the cities, Cherriots, TriMet, ODOT, both counties and Portland & Western. Today the same trip is served by the SMART and Cherriots 1X bus, carrying a few hundred riders a day. Supporters themselves estimate fifteen years or more from serious start to service.

2022 to 2023 · Sketched

A Salem streetcar, and the West Salem connector

Proposals to study a Salem streetcar have surfaced repeatedly, including a West Salem connector concept that would carry track across the 1912 Union Street Railroad Bridge and fly over the rail line and Front Street into downtown. The bridge last carried trains in the early 1990s, was sold to the city for a dollar in 2003, and has been a much-loved walking and cycling crossing since 2009.

What the record says

Four lessons we would rather learn from someone else's expense

  • Rail without density underneath it does not fill. WES connects suburb to suburb, through farmland and industrial land, and the destination employment never materialized around it. This is the central warning, and it points directly at what a district developer can actually contribute.
  • Sharing a freight corridor constrains the timetable. WES runs peak-hours only on weekdays, partly because a shared line makes frequent, regular service hard. Any honest version of this concept treats scheduling as the hard problem, not an afterthought.
  • Vehicle and staffing rules drive operating cost. Federal requirements on crewing and equipment are a large part of why a WES trip costs what it does. Lighter, newer vehicle classes exist partly to change that arithmetic, which is why the technology generations on this page matter beyond novelty.
  • The state has already chosen incrementalism. Oregon studied building new alignment and decided against it, in favor of improving what exists, in pieces. A corridor-reuse hypothesis is aligned with adopted state policy rather than arguing against it.
Read together, the record suggests the sequence usually runs backwards. Build the rail, then hope the development and the riders follow. Edgewater offers the reverse: the development is happening regardless, which means the riders arrive before the service has to justify itself. That inversion is the only genuinely new thing we bring to a very old question.
The Geography

The valley is the market

Transportation changes land value because it changes what is reachable. Salem sits between Oregon's largest metropolitan economy to the north and two major university cities to the south. A functioning regional connection would begin to move Edgewater out of a purely Salem frame of reference and into the economic geography of the whole valley.

Today

West Salem's connection to the rest of the city runs through a small number of bridges. Most trips require a car. The regional connection is I-5, and it behaves like every other American interstate at rush hour.

Potentially

A rail connection that treats West Salem as a starting point rather than a suburb, with the district's mobility hub as the transfer point between regional travel and local movement.

The Stack

Four layers, and Amtrak is one of them

This is not a proposal to compete with the passenger rail Oregon already funds. Amtrak Cascades is the express layer of this picture, and a regional service would exist to feed it, not to duplicate it. The layers below get progressively more local, and each one makes the others more useful.

Express
Amtrak Cascades

Already running, ODOT sponsored, connecting Eugene, Albany, Salem and Portland onward to Seattle and Vancouver. Intercity distances at intercity frequencies. An asset to connect to, and the reason a regional layer does not need to solve long-distance travel.

Regional
Willamette Valley Electric

The layer that does not exist today: more stations, higher frequency, smaller battery-electric vehicles on existing corridors where feasible, and eventually increasing automation. This is the hypothesis this page is about.

Local
Whoosh and autonomous transit

Station to destination, across the river, around the district and through the city. Frequent, on demand, and the reason a regional trip ends somewhere useful. Described in full on the Whoosh and Salem in Motion pages.

Human scale
Walk, bike, micromobility

The last few minutes, which decide whether any of the layers above get used at all.

The systems make each other better, which is the actual argument. Regional service is only as useful as what you can do when you step off it, so a local circulator raises the value of every arriving train. The circulator is only as useful as the density around its stops, so development raises its value. And a district built around both needs less parking, which changes what can be built at all.

The goal is not to replace a car with a different vehicle you have to own. It is a network dense enough that most trips stop requiring one.

The Technology

What is actually rolling right now

The reason to raise this question in 2026 rather than 2006 is that the vehicles changed. Rail is going through what road freight and passenger cars went through a decade ago: batteries first, then autonomy. Below is what exists, who is building it, and how far along each one honestly is.

Schematic, drawn to relative scale. The trend runs from fewer, larger, scheduled vehicles toward more, smaller, responsive ones.

Generation one

Battery passenger rail

Pop-Up Metro, a Railroad Development Corporation company now merged with the UK's TDI Greenway, puts remanufactured battery trainsets on lightly used freight lines with simple platforms and almost no new infrastructure. The pitch is explicitly about de-risking: prove the demand before committing to conventional commuter rail. Two battery trainsets are in demonstration operation on a Pennsylvania test railroad.

Demonstration ready today
Generation two

Very light rail

Coventry Very Light Rail in the UK is the furthest along of the lightweight programmes: a battery vehicle carrying around fifty passengers on a shallow track slab that avoids most utility relocation, with a demonstrator section completed in 2025 and passenger service targeted for 2026 or 2027. The programme's stated aim is roughly half the cost and half the build time of conventional light rail.

In public testing
Generation three

Autonomous battery vehicles

Parallel Systems builds small autonomous battery-electric rail vehicles that travel individually or in platoons of ten to thirty rather than as coupled trains. After Federal Railroad Administration approval, the company began a commercial pilot across roughly 160 miles of Georgia railroad, has tested compatibility with existing train control alongside a Class I railroad, and raised a thirty-eight million dollar round to scale production.

Commercial freight pilot running
The connection worth knowing. Parallel Systems' pilot partner is Genesee & Wyoming. Portland & Western, the regional railroad operating in this valley with executive offices in Salem, is a Genesee & Wyoming company, and it is also the railroad that operates Oregon's only commuter rail service. That is not an introduction, an agreement, or a plan. It means the people who would have to say yes are already in Oregon, already running passenger trains on a shared freight corridor, and already inside the corporate family piloting autonomous battery rail in America.

Further out, and labeled honestly

European research programmes including Urbanloop, TaxiRail and Pods4Rail are testing individually routed, on-demand rail pods: closer to an elevator network than a timetable. They are genuinely interesting and genuinely unproven at scale. We list them as research rather than options, and we would not plan a corridor around them. What they indicate is direction of travel, and the direction is smaller vehicles arriving more often.

Notice what none of these require: new right of way, electrification wires, or a technology that has not left a laboratory. That is the whole point. The corridor is the expensive part, and it already exists.

The Position

Connect the valley's brains

Look at the map again and a second argument appears, one that is about economies rather than trains. Salem sits at the center of a line that runs from Oregon's largest metropolitan economy through the state capital to two major research universities.

Portland

Technology, capital, employment, and an international airport.

Salem

State government, industry, a growing population, and a district being planned around mobility.

Corvallis

Oregon State University: engineering, robotics, materials, and research that needs somewhere to be commercialized.

Eugene

The University of Oregon, and the southern anchor of the valley economy.

Better regional connectivity between those places could plausibly widen labor markets, make university collaboration and research commercialization easier, give students and employers more options, and expand the catchment for culture, tourism and events. We are deliberately not attaching numbers to any of that, because credible numbers come from a study rather than a website.

What it does change is how to describe this project. Edgewater is not simply a mixed-use district in West Salem. It could be a mobility-oriented innovation district near the center of a Portland to Eugene economic corridor. That is a different proposition for an employer, a university, or an investor.

An important piece of realism. The viable north-south corridor may well sit on the east side of Salem, and forcing regional rail across the Willamette into Edgewater would be expensive and probably unnecessary. A fast, frequent local link across the river can deliver most of the accessibility benefit. The objective is not to put a train through Edgewater. It is for Edgewater to function as though the regional network begins at its front door.
The Constraint

Passengers and freight, not passengers instead of freight

The most common way a concept like this fails is by treating the railroad's existing business as an obstacle. The corridor performs an economic function today, its customers depend on it, and no serious proposal begins by proposing to take it away.

The relevant idea is temporal separation, and it is the same idea Pop-Up Metro built its model on: freight moves at night and in defined windows, passengers move during the day, and the corridor does two jobs on one set of rails. Whether that works on this corridor depends on things we do not yet know, including freight volumes and timing, track condition, signaling, passing capacity, and the railroad's own appetite.

Operating strategies worth studying, in roughly the order they would be studied: scheduled freight and passenger windows, signaling modernization, dispatch integration, selective passing infrastructure or double tracking, freight consolidation, and eventually battery or autonomous freight operations that change the economics for the railroad as well.

Stated plainly: a shared corridor has to be worth more to the railroad than an exclusive one. If it is not, the concept fails at the first meeting, and it should.

The Value

Mobility creates place value

Accessibility is the mechanism. When more of a region is reachable without a car, the ground under a district behaves differently, and the effects compound in a loop.

Transit accessibility has historically created the conditions for a larger effective labor market, lower household transportation costs, reduced parking requirements, greater development capacity, stronger retail foot traffic, larger visitor catchments and stronger station-area economics. Those conditions tend to show up in land value.

We are deliberately not putting a number on that here. Any figure produced before a feasibility study would be invented, and this is a hypothesis to be modeled during predevelopment rather than a projected outcome. What the flywheel does justify is studying the corridor early, because if it works, it changes the underwriting of everything built around it.

Funding mechanisms worth studying

Tax increment and urban renewal, station area and joint development, development agreements, infrastructure districts, public-private partnerships, land value capture, federal and state transportation funding, clean mobility and demonstration grants, and technology partnerships. None of these is committed, applied for, or approved. They are the menu a feasibility study would price.

The Path

Start small. Prove it. Expand.

The fastest way to discredit this idea would be to open with Portland. The credible sequence starts with questions, then a short demonstration, and earns the right to each following step.

Phase 0

Corridor discovery

Ownership, operating rights, freight customers and volumes, track and bridge condition, crossings, signaling, right of way width, station opportunities, regulatory jurisdiction, and connections to existing transit. Mostly desk work and conversations. Cheap, and it either opens the door or closes it honestly.

Phase 1

A Salem demonstration

A short segment that tests vehicle technology, operating compatibility with freight, station design, charging, community response and actual ridership. Edgewater is the natural hub for it, because the district is being built anyway and can host the stop, the charging and the crowd.

Phase 2

A Salem and Keizer spine

Extend to a useful passenger catchment and integrate properly with Cherriots, cycling infrastructure and the district circulator, so the rail line is part of a network rather than a novelty.

Phase 3

Regional connection

Explore extension along the valley: north toward Wilsonville and the Portland metro, south toward Albany and from there to Corvallis and Eugene, with Amtrak Cascades as the express spine rather than a competitor. This is the phase everyone wants to start with, and the one that only works if the earlier phases have proven demand.

Phase 4

An autonomous corridor

As regulation and technology mature, smaller vehicles at higher frequency with increasing automation. The corridor does not need to be rebuilt for this. The vehicles change; the infrastructure stays.

The destination is not a train. It is a transportation protocol running on rail infrastructure, where the vehicles can evolve without rebuilding the corridor.

Open Questions

What we do not know yet

This page would be worth less if it pretended otherwise. These are the questions Phase 0 exists to answer, and any one of them could end the idea.

Who holds the rightsOwnership of the line, operating rights, and what a passenger operator would actually need to negotiate.
What the freight business needsCurrent customers, volumes, timing, and what a shared corridor would have to be worth to the railroad.
What the track can takeCondition, bridges, crossings, signaling, and the cost of bringing any of it to passenger standard.
Who regulates itFederal, state and local jurisdiction, and what approvals a demonstration would actually require.
Whether anyone would rideHonest demand estimates for the segments that could realistically run first.
What it costsCapital and operating cost for a demonstration, and who would fund and operate it.

Partners who would need to be part of answering these: the City of Salem, ODOT, SKATS and the regional council, Cherriots, Portland & Western, Oregon State University and other institutions, mobility technology companies, and the landowners and employers along the corridor. We are one participant among many, and on most of these questions we are not the ones with the answers.

The Larger Idea

A nineteenth-century corridor, a twenty-first-century system

For a century, railroads decided where American cities grew. Then we stopped building around them, and in most places the corridors quietly kept running with a fraction of the traffic they were built for. The infrastructure is still there. Battery propulsion, lighter vehicles, automation and better networks now make it reasonable to ask a question that would have sounded naive twenty years ago.

What if the railroad became city-building infrastructure again? Salem has the corridor, a district being planned around it, and a regional railroad headquartered in town. That is an unusually good place to find out.

The strategy is deliberately the inverse of how these things usually fail. Not a network, then an enormous capital program, then hope development follows. Instead: a place, then a demonstration, then a corridor, then a network, with each step earning the next. Big systems can start with small places, and this one would start here.

Mobility companies

A district willing to host a demonstration, with a development team that can build the stop, the charging and the ridership around it.

Public agencies

A privately funded first study of a corridor question the region will eventually have to answer anyway.

Railroads and investors

A conversation about whether a shared corridor is worth more than an exclusive one, and what it would take to find out.
