The Ambition

What would it take
to make Salem the most
liveable city in America?

Not a promise. A question we think is worth asking out loud, with an honest account of what Salem already has, what it does not, and what one district can and cannot do about it.

A civic agenda · Edgewater as a first move · An open invitation
The Question

We asked the bigger version first, and published the answer

Before writing any of this down, we ran the comparison that everyone reaches for: could Salem be the most liveable city in the world, the way Copenhagen is? We published the answer on this site, and it was no.

Why not the world

Global livability indices are decided largely by things a city government does not control: healthcare systems, violent crime, national safety nets. No American metro reaches the global top twenty, and the drag is stability and health, not schools or planning. Any pitch implying otherwise is selling a ranking it does not understand.

Why America is the real question

Strip out the national moat and what remains is exactly the local ledger: land use, density, the public realm, the riverfront, transit and cycling, tree canopy, housing supply, and the discipline to hold a plan for decades. Those are the levers a city and region actually hold, and they are the ones that decide who leads in America.

The full analysis, including where the parallels hold and where they break, is in The Copenhagen Comparison. This page picks up where that one leaves off.

A shrinking, broke, polluted industrial city chose differently, and kept choosing for forty years. Trajectory is a choice.

What It Means

Liveability is measured in ordinary days

Not in awards. A home you can afford near the things you love. Trips that work without a car. Streets that hold public life. Buildings that keep you healthy. Nature doing real work. Culture, care, and play within a short walk. These are not amenities in the shallow sense; they are the daily experience of a place, and they drive demand, retention, health, and long-term value in that order.

For residents

Lower cost of living when a household can drop a car. Shorter trips. More daylight, more movement, more neighbors. The measurable stuff follows the felt stuff.

For the city

A more diversified tax base per acre, infrastructure that serves more people per dollar, and the kind of place that keeps graduates and attracts employers rather than exporting both.

For the region

Housing supply where the jobs already are, farmland protected by growing in rather than out, and a template the next Willamette Valley city can borrow.

What Salem Has

The raw material is unusually good

This is not a city starting from nothing. Most of what makes liveability possible is already here, which is precisely why the question is worth asking.

  • A planning tradition. Oregon's 1973 land-use system and its urban growth boundaries are the closest thing in America to European planning discipline. Growing in rather than out is state policy here, not a fight.
  • A capital city's institutions. Government, universities, hospitals, and a stable employment base that does not evaporate in a downturn.
  • A river and a valley. A working waterfront to reclaim, and nature in every direction within an hour: coast, mountains, forests, farms, and wine country.
  • Room, and relative affordability. Land that can still be shaped, at prices that make attainable housing arithmetically possible, an hour from a major metro.
  • Momentum. Oregon ranked first in the nation for inbound movers in 2025. People are already choosing this valley.
What Is Missing

And the honest other column

A page that only lists strengths is marketing. Here is the gap between Salem today and the question at the top of this page.

  • Walkable density, almost anywhere. Very few places in the city where daily needs are genuinely reachable on foot, which is the precondition for everything else.
  • An activated riverfront. The Willamette runs through the middle of the city and is barely part of daily life on the west side.
  • Delivery capacity. Ambitious projects need teams, capital, and entitlement paths that a mid-size city does not automatically have. This is the constraint people underestimate most.
  • Housing supply, at the right prices. Not a Salem problem alone, but the shortage lands hardest in exactly this kind of city.
  • A destination reason to cross the river. West Salem is minutes from downtown and functions like a different city.
What A District Can Do

Edgewater is a first move, not the answer

Twenty-two riverfront acres cannot make a city the most liveable in America. What a district can do is prove the method, and give everything that follows something real to point at.

Proof at a walkable scale

One neighborhood where the whole argument is built at once: homes, work, food, wellness, nature, and mobility sharing the same few acres, and performance measured and published rather than asserted.

Housing where it counts

A meaningful number of attainable homes delivered on underused land inside the growth boundary, which is the least contested housing any city can add.

The riverfront, made public

A working stretch of waterfront turned into somewhere people actually go, which is how Copenhagen's harbor story started too.

Delivery capacity, built once

The teams, the capital structures, and the entitlement experience assembled for this district are reusable by the city long after it opens. That capability is arguably the bigger civic asset.

What It Cannot

The limits, stated plainly

We would rather name these than be caught implying otherwise. A private development, however well intentioned, does not get to decide a city's future.

  • It cannot build a citywide transit or bike network. That is the city, the county, and the state, over years.
  • It cannot fix schools, healthcare access, or public safety, which weigh heavily in how anyone measures liveability.
  • It cannot substitute for housing policy. One district's homes matter; the citywide supply question is decided in the code and at the council.
  • It cannot represent the community. We are one participant among many, and the people who already live here have the deciding voice on what Salem becomes.
  • It cannot move quickly. This is a decade of work, and the honest version says so up front.

The parts that are not ours to do

Why Capital Cares

Liveability is the demand thesis

For investors reading this: the civic ambition and the return case are the same argument told from two directions.

Demand

Walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods are chronically undersupplied in America, and the shortage is sharpest in growing mid-size cities. Scarcity of the product is the durable part of the thesis.

Durability

Walkable urbanism trades at a premium that tends to widen as a district matures, while conventional product depreciates against its own comps. The public realm is the asset.

Risk

A district aligned with the city's own goals moves faster through entitlement, attracts public and philanthropic capital, and holds community support. Alignment is a risk-management strategy, not a soft one.
