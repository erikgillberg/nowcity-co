Working Strategy · Capital

Milestone-based
development finance

A development project gets safer every month. Its capital almost never gets cheaper. This is why that gap exists, roughly what it costs, and what would have to change to close it.

Working notes · West Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Gap

Risk falls in steps. Price holds flat.

Every development project retires risk in a sequence of discrete, knowable events, and almost none of that shows up in what the capital costs. A site with no entitlement is a different asset from the same site with an approved plan. A building with a signed anchor lease is a different asset from an empty one. The market knows this. It just prices the whole journey once, at the beginning, when the project is at its most dangerous, and then leaves the price there.

What actually happens to risk

Land is optioned. Title comes back clean. Environmental comes back clean. The zone change carries. The city commits to the infrastructure. A guaranteed maximum price is signed. An anchor commits to space. Construction tops out. The certificate of occupancy issues. Lease-up hits stabilization. Each of those is a specific, dated, verifiable event that removes a specific category of loss.

What happens to price

Almost nothing, until a refinancing. The sponsor pays for entitlement risk long after entitlement is granted, and for lease-up risk long after the building is full. The saving arrives in one lump at the end, if the sponsor survives long enough to refinance. That lump is real money, and for a phased district it is the difference between a project that compounds and one that stalls between phases.

Why It Persists

Proving a milestone costs more than repricing it saves

This is not a failure of imagination. It is an arithmetic problem, and it has been the right answer for a long time. Repricing a facility mid-term means a lender has to be persuaded, in a form its credit committee accepts, that something specific has changed. That persuasion has a cost, and until recently the cost exceeded the saving on anything but the largest deals.

Verification is manual

A milestone is proved with documents, opinions, site visits and a memo. Every one of those is somebody's billable time, and the same fact often gets proved separately to the senior lender, the mezzanine lender and the equity.

Loan documents are static

Most credit agreements fix pricing for the term. Changing it is an amendment, which means counsel on both sides and a return to committee. The transaction cost of the change can exceed a year of the saving.

Nobody trusts the sponsor's word

And they are right not to. The party who benefits from the milestone is the party asserting it happened. Without an independent, disinterested certifier, a claimed milestone is just a claim.

So the interesting question is not whether risk-adjusted pricing should exist. It is what happens to development finance when the cost of proving a milestone falls far enough that repricing becomes economic on a mid-size project rather than only on a very large one.

Precedent

The mechanism is ordinary everywhere except here

Nothing about pricing that moves with verified performance is exotic. It is standard in several other corners of credit, which is the best argument that it can work in this one.

Corporate pricing grids

Leveraged loans have carried margin ratchets tied to leverage ratios for decades. The borrower delivers a compliance certificate, the ratio is checked, the margin steps. Nobody considers this novel.

Sustainability-linked loans

A whole asset class now prices to verified, third-party-audited performance targets, with the margin stepping up or down against them. The verification apparatus was built in about a decade.

Construction to permanent conversion

Real estate already accepts that pricing should fall at one milestone, stabilization. The principle is conceded. What is missing is the granularity.

Delayed-draw and milestone tranches

Venture debt and project finance both release capital against defined events rather than in a lump. Again, the gating exists. The pricing simply does not move with it.

The pattern across all four is the same. Where verification is cheap, standardized and independent, pricing follows performance. Where it is expensive and bespoke, pricing is set once and held. Development finance sits firmly in the second category, and there is no structural reason it has to.

The Early Stage

A published study is a form of credit enhancement

The most expensive capital in development is the earliest, and the reason is almost entirely informational. At Stage 0 nobody knows whether the market is there, whether the site works, or whether the plan is permittable, and the sponsor is the only party asserting any of it. Capital prices that ignorance, which is why predevelopment money is priced closer to venture than to real estate.

What a study actually does

An independent feasibility study, commissioned by a public body and published, retires the sponsor-credibility problem for every capital provider who comes afterward. It is diligence performed once, by a disinterested party, at public expense, and available to everyone. The second investor does not have to repeat the first investor's work.

Seen that way, grant-funded feasibility work is not a subsidy to a developer. It is a piece of shared market infrastructure, and it is the cheapest credit enhancement available anywhere in the capital stack.

Why that is a public argument, not a private one

If a study is paid for privately it belongs to the sponsor and everyone else discounts it. If it is paid for publicly and published openly, it is trusted, and its value accrues to every subsequent project in the same market rather than to one balance sheet.

This is the honest answer to why a public body should fund the work described on our Oregon homes and innovation district pages. Not because a developer wants it. Because verified information is a public good that lowers the cost of capital for everybody who builds here next.

The first rung of a pricing ladder is not a financial instrument at all. It is a document, produced by somebody with no stake in the answer, that anyone can read.

The Sharpest Case

Making factory progress bankable

There is one place where milestone verification is worth far more than a few basis points, and it is the constraint that has killed most of the industrialized housing companies of the last decade.

The problem

Sixty to eighty percent of a factory-built building's value is completed inside a plant, months before anything appears on site. Construction lenders release draws against visible site progress. So the manufacturer finances the work in progress itself, and that working capital demand is what most of them ran out of.

Why it is a verification problem

The lender is not being unreasonable. It cannot see the collateral, it may not have a perfected security interest in goods sitting on a third party's floor, and it has no standard way to confirm what is finished. Every one of those is solvable with a certifier, a protocol and the right documents.

What would fix it

An independent engineer stationed at the factory, a defined completion standard per component, an attested record the lender can rely on, bailee and access agreements over the goods, and a draw mechanism that suspends automatically on a disputed certification.

Instruments that do exactly this are now being built. Programmable guarantee and settlement layers exist that sit on top of conventional bank messaging, gate disbursement to independently certified milestones, hold a challenge window before a certification becomes final, and give the issuing bank documented grounds to reprice as the risk retires. We have looked at one in some detail. The mechanism is sound and the fit with off-site production is the most compelling use we have seen for it, which is a more interesting claim than generic fee compression.
This is also, and not by coincidence, the third of the five constraints on the Oregon homes strategy page, and the one we described there as something a state could solve once for every manufacturer rather than every manufacturer solving alone. A standing facility that advances against verified factory progress is a policy instrument as much as a private one.
Limits

What we would not claim

The failure mode of this idea is to describe it as automatic, and it is not. Five things we would say plainly to any lender or investor before saying anything else.

  1. Pricing authority stays with the lender. Verified milestones create documented grounds to reprice. They do not compel it. Anyone describing automatic fee compression is describing a negotiation, not a mechanism, and the difference matters.
  2. Credit enhancement is not free. A guarantee wrap carries a fee that sits on top of the debt cost, and at small facility sizes that fee can exceed what the compression returns. It earns its place only where it unlocks leverage or pricing otherwise unavailable.
  3. The certifier is the whole thing. Every claim here rests on an independent party with professional liability and no stake in the answer. Cryptography makes a record tamper-evident. It does not make the underlying assertion true.
  4. Novel instruments carry their own risk premium. Being the first live deployment of anything is expensive. We would rather be the second or third, on a structure a bank has already closed and a regulator has already seen.
  5. None of this substitutes for the project working. A perfectly instrumented capital stack on a project nobody wants to live in is a well-documented failure. The ladder above is worth building only because we think the underlying development is worth building.
Where We Are

What we would want to test first

Write the ladder with a lender

Take the table above to two or three lenders and ask which rows they would actually price, which they would not, and what evidence they would need. That conversation is worth having before any instrument is chosen.

Price the off-site question

Establish what it would take for a construction lender to advance against certified factory progress on a real facility: the certifier, the completion standard, the security package, the documents. This is the piece with the largest prize attached.

Fund the first rung publicly

The independent, published feasibility study is the cheapest and most valuable milestone on the ladder, and it is the one a public body should pay for. It is already the near-term ask on our other pages.
