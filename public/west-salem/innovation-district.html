District Plan · Innovation District

Where the next
economy is made

A plan to initiate an applied-technology district on the Willamette riverfront - a place where hard things get built, tested, and grown.

A planned initiative · Modeled on Newlab at the Brooklyn Navy Yard · West Salem, Oregon
The Idea

An innovation district we intend to build, not one we claim to have

Edgewater is more than housing. The plan is to convene and initiate an Edgewater Innovation District: a working community where founders, engineers, researchers, and makers develop applied technology in the open, on a real riverfront, alongside the people who live and work there. We are at the beginning of that work. This page is a plan and an invitation, built on how comparable districts were actually started elsewhere, and on the partners we intend to bring to the table to make it real.

The most durable innovation districts were not announced into existence. They were a mission-driven operator, one anchor building, shared tools, a public partner, and a first project that proved the idea.

Now City intends to initiate this district in partnership with universities, enterprise, government, philanthropy, and the West Salem community. None of these partnerships are in place yet. They are the work ahead, and the plan below describes how we propose to do it.
The Model

Newlab, inside the Brooklyn Navy Yard

The clearest model for what we intend is Newlab: a venture and maker community for hard technology, housed in one rehabilitated industrial building inside a much larger working campus. It is not a co-working space. It lowers three barriers that stop hardware founders, infrastructure to prototype, real projects to pilot, and access to capital, and it sits inside a city-owned industrial yard run for jobs rather than rent. That nested structure, a curated maker building inside a mission-run campus, is exactly the shape Edgewater can take.

What Newlab is

Founded 2016 by David Belt and Scott Cohen, modeled on the MIT Media Lab, in Building 128, an 1899 Navy machine shop rebuilt into roughly 84,000 square feet of studios, labs, and a full fabrication shop, metal, wood, 3D printing, electronics, and CNC.

How it was built

A public landlord owned and rehabbed the shell; a private operator brought the vision and curation. The roughly $82.8M Building 128 project was funded with $25M of New Markets Tax Credits plus historic tax credits, federal, state, and city grants, and private capital. No single balance sheet carried it.

Why it works

Shared tools, a curated community, and real-world pilots compound. Newlab reports more than 300 member startups and over 1,000 members across its locations, and it has since opened a second hub at Michigan Central in Detroit with Ford. The model is portable.

An honest note on the numbers. The Building 128 capital stack ($25M of New Markets Tax Credits on a roughly $82.8M project) is documented by the New Markets Tax Credit Coalition. Newlab's member counts and the capital its companies have raised are largely self-reported and vary across its own pages, so we treat them as orders of magnitude, not audited facts.
The Host Campus

The Yard is the lesson, not just the building

Newlab matters because of where it sits. The Brooklyn Navy Yard is a 300-acre, city-owned former shipyard, run by a non-profit development corporation on behalf of New York City to a jobs-and-equity mandate. Today it reports more than 550 businesses, over 13,000 jobs, and more than $2.5B in annual economic impact, with an on-site employment center that has placed thousands of local residents and a career-and-technical high school on campus. The innovation building does not have to carry the whole district. It anchors a larger working place.

Why Here

Salem already sits in an innovation corridor

The thesis is not that West Salem must invent an economy from nothing. It is that Salem sits inside a real and growing Willamette Valley innovation corridor, and the district can give that corridor a front door on the river. The credibility below is current and verifiable; it is the regional momentum we intend to plug into, not something the district has captured.

A world-first anchor, in Salem

Agility Robotics' RoboFab in Salem is described as the world's first purpose-built humanoid-robot factory, around 70,000 SF, designed to scale beyond 10,000 robots a year and 500-plus jobs. The company, spun out of Oregon State, closed a $400M round in March 2025. Advanced manufacturing at global scale is already here.

Federal Tech Hubs next door

In 2023 the U.S. Department of Commerce designated two Oregon State-led clusters as federal Tech Hubs, in semiconductors and in mass timber, competitive national designations. The research engine for an applied district is 40 miles south in Corvallis.

A clean-construction economy

Oregon's mass-timber sector, the OSU and University of Oregon TallWood Design Institute, and the Port of Portland's mass-timber manufacturing campus show a Pacific Northwest economy converting industrial waterfronts into clean-construction innovation. West Salem can be a node in it.

Sources: Agility Robotics and Salem Reporter; U.S. EDA and OPB on the Oregon Tech Hubs; the Oregon Mass Timber Coalition and the Port of Portland. The district plans to connect to this corridor; it has not yet secured any of these relationships.
The Anchor · Our Building 128

The bow-truss campus is the building this district is waiting for

Edgewater already has its Building 128. The 400 Patterson campus is three 40,000 SF bow-truss warehouses on the riverfront, the kind of raw, high-bay industrial structure that maker districts are built inside. Our Activation Plan already proposes leasing and converting these into live-work lofts and innovation space under an operating-to-property-company structure. The Innovation District gives that move its purpose: the bow-truss campus becomes the curated maker and venture building at the heart of the district, with shared fabrication, demonstration space, and a pipeline of founders.

Adaptive reuse is the authentic move

Newlab, Michigan Central, and Greentown Labs all began in old industrial buildings. The story is genuine and it is tax-advantaged: a historic or industrial rehab can unlock historic and New Markets tax credits that help close the gap, exactly as Building 128 did. The bow-truss campus lets Edgewater tell the same true story on the Willamette.

The district is the real-world lab

The single biggest advantage of building this inside a new mixed-use neighborhood is that founders can pilot in the wild, in streets, homes, energy, water, food, and mobility systems that real residents use. Detroit proved the multiplier when a designated innovation and testing zone filled the building in months. Edgewater can offer the same: a place to deploy, not just to prototype.

The Operating System

Four parts, drawn from how these districts actually formed

Across every precedent we studied, the pattern that made districts real was structural, not aspirational. Four parts have to be present. Each has a clear Edgewater analog, and naming them is how we keep the plan honest about what we still have to build.

01 · A mission-driven operator

A neutral entity that holds the land and writes a jobs-and-community mandate into the leases. In Brooklyn that is BNYDC. Here it is Now City's operating-to-property-company model, with the City of Salem and its urban-renewal agency as the public partner.

02 · An anchor maker building

One signature, adaptive-reuse building inside a larger working district. The bow-truss 400 Patterson campus is ours, ready to become shops, studios, labs, and demonstration space.

03 · Shared tools + a testbed

Fabrication and prototyping no single startup could afford, plus a real neighborhood to pilot in. The district itself, its streets, energy, water, food, and mobility, is the proving ground.

04 · A blended capital stack

Not one private balance sheet, but an operator plus public partners stacking the tools that fit: New Markets and historic tax credits, EDA and state grants, and patient anchor capital. The Building 128 playbook, adapted to Oregon.

A Focus, Not Everything

A district earns identity by owning a theme

Greentown Labs became a national magnet by owning climate technology; Newlab built around critical technologies it could test in place. Edgewater should pick a focus it can credibly own in the Willamette Valley rather than promise to incubate everything. The strongest candidates sit at the intersection of the region's real strengths and the district's living-lab advantage. These are directions to validate with partners, not commitments.

Food & agri-tech

A living testbed for regenerative agriculture, food logistics, and circular food systems, building on the agri-food innovation framework already in the District Vision and the valley's farm economy.

Water & river systems

A Willamette-front district is a natural place to develop and demonstrate stormwater, reuse, and river-health technology, in the wild, beside the water it protects.

Clean energy & buildings

High-performance and mass-timber construction, district energy, and building-tech piloted in the homes and shops being built around the maker campus.

Mobility

The car-light district and its planned circulator make Edgewater a real-world mobility lab, the cluster already sketched in the Salem Mobility Concepts.

These connect directly to work already in the plan: the agri-food framework in the District Vision and the mobility cluster in the Salem Mobility Concepts. The Innovation District is the structure that ties them together.
Precedents

Where this has actually worked, and the one lesson from each

Newlab is the lead model, but it is one of many. Each precedent below contributes a single transferable lesson, and together they describe a path that is well-worn rather than novel.

Brooklyn, NY

Brooklyn Navy Yard

A city-owned shipyard turned into a 300-acre working campus by a mission-driven non-profit landlord over decades.

Lesson: a neutral operator with a jobs mandate, not a rent-maximizer, is what makes a district last.
Detroit, MI

Michigan Central + Newlab

Ford rebuilt a derelict train station into a 30-acre district; Newlab operates the venture platform inside a designated testing zone. It reached 100-plus startups in 14 months.

Lesson: an anchor partner supplying real pilots fills a maker building fast.
Somerville, MA

Greentown Labs

North America's largest climate-tech incubator, a non-profit in a rehabbed 1908 industrial building with shared wet labs and prototyping.

Lesson: own one theme deeply and become its national magnet.
San Francisco, CA

Pier 70 / Dogpatch

A historic industrial waterfront converted to a mixed-use district through a partnership between a master developer and the public landowner (the Port).

Lesson: a public landowner plus a master developer can transform a riverfront at scale.
Toronto, ON

MaRS Discovery District

A large urban innovation hub that acts as a single front door convening startups, corporates, investors, and government.

Lesson: a visible front-door institution does the convening a scattered ecosystem cannot.
Skive, Denmark

GreenLab

A green industrial park where co-located energy producers and users share surplus power and resources, founded by Christopher Sorensen as a public-private partnership in a small rural municipality.

Lesson: a shared infrastructure backbone can turn a business park into a system. One of several references, not our template.
The Engagement Plan

Who has to be at the table, and in what order

A district like this is convened, not declared. The precedents are consistent: start with a small anchor-plus-public-partner coalition, vest a neutral operator, secure the enabling powers, and prove it with one financeable project before widening the tent. Below is who we intend to bring in, what each brings, and the sequence we propose to follow.

Phase 0
Anchor + public partner

Lock a credible anchor and one public partner, the City of Salem, with SEDCOR as facilitator. Keep the room small.

Phase 1
Coalition + MOU

Add the research, workforce, and state legs. Sign a non-binding MOU defining the shared vision, roles, and the neutral operator.

Phase 2
Stand up the operator

Designate or charter the neutral entity that holds relationships, coordinates permitting, and recruits, our BNYDC role.

Phase 3
Secure the powers

With the City and Business Oregon, line up the friction-reducers: urban-renewal or TIF, enterprise or opportunity-zone overlays, expedited permitting, tax-credit eligibility.

Phase 4
Prove it, then widen

Land one financeable demonstration, the maker building plus its first cohort, then broaden to community, tribal, and philanthropic partners with a visible benefit track.

Where We Are

Planning, not promising

It matters that this reads as the honest plan it is. There is no operating innovation hub on the West Salem riverfront today, no signed university or anchor partnership, and no committed public capital stack. What exists is a credible site with its own Building 128, a well-evidenced model, a regional corridor worth connecting to, and a sequence for convening the partners who can make it real. We would rather show that path plainly than overstate what is already here. The next step is the first conversation in Phase 0.

What we are not claiming. We are not claiming an existing innovation district, secured partnerships, or guaranteed funding. We are presenting a plan grounded in real precedents and a real asset, and inviting the partners named above to help build it.
