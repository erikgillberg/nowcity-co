District Plan · Innovation District

Oregon as a hub
for physical AI

Robotics, agriculture, health care, mobility and construction share the same underlying engineering: perception, actuation, control, safety validation, and manufacturing at low volume. Oregon has strength across those components. This page argues they should be developed together rather than separately, and that the district should be judged by whether daily life in it improves.

Working notes · West Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Premise

What the technology is for

None of this technology exists to reward investors, though it will. It exists to help us get faster to communities that are resilient, regenerative and worth living in. That is the test we would like to be held to, and it is why we are building a neighborhood rather than a campus. The only way to know whether machines that move through the physical world actually improve daily life is to build them where people live, and then measure it.

The technical argument sits underneath. Robotics, agriculture, care, mobility and construction are treated as separate industries. From the engineering side they share one stack: perception, actuation, control, safety validation, and manufacturing at low volume. Oregon has unusual depth in the components of that stack, spread across a semiconductor cluster, a robotics lineage at Oregon State, one of the most diverse farm valleys on earth, a regional health system, and a mass-timber sector.

The hypothesis this district tests is that the hard problems in physical AI live at the interfaces between those components, that teams working inside a single application silo meet them late and expensively, and that putting the applications next to each other surfaces them early while they are still cheap. What follows is the evidence from districts that worked, the evidence from districts that disappointed, and what we do not yet know.

Why
Oregon holds the components of the physical AI stack across a semiconductor cluster, a robotics research lineage, a working farm valley and a health system. What it lacks is one place where they meet.
What
The same engineering applied to five domains, with shared fabrication and instrumentation as the common infrastructure, in the spirit of Newlab at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, set inside a West Salem neighborhood where the work can also be deployed.
How
The proven coalition playbook: a university, government, industry, and community, held by a neutral trust, anchored by adaptive reuse and urban-renewal financing.
When
A clear five-move sequence: convene a champion, see a working district together, form the trust, land the anchor and the public tool, then build the first proof.
Measure
We do not know yet. What we will commit to is what gets counted: whether local incomes, ownership, small-business survival and talent retention actually move.
Why Here

What Oregon already has

Oregon's Silicon Forest is one of the deepest technology ecosystems in the country: more than 6,000 high-tech businesses and 81,000 jobs, running from Intel and the semiconductor cluster around Portland through instrumentation, software and research talent down the length of the Willamette. Salem sits on that corridor. What the valley does not have is a single place where these industries work near each other.

Humanoid manufacturing in Salem

Agility Robotics, an Oregon State spinout, manufactures humanoid robots at RoboFab in Salem, reported as the first purpose-built factory of its kind. We have no relationship with the company and describe it here only as evidence that this work is already happening in the valley.

Two federal Tech Hubs

Oregon State leads two federally designated Tech Hubs, in semiconductors and in mass timber, among the few universities in the country to hold two. The research engine for an applied district sits 35 miles south.

Mass timber and clean construction

Oregon's mass-timber sector, the OSU and University of Oregon TallWood Design Institute, and the Port of Portland's timber manufacturing campus show a region turning industrial waterfronts into clean-construction innovation. Edgewater joins that map.

The Argument

The shared engineering

Most innovation districts pick one industry. The argument here is that these five are better understood as five applications of the same engineering. A weeding robot, a care assistant, an autonomous shuttle and a construction arm share perception, actuation, control, safety validation, and the problem of building a hundred units rather than a million. What differs is the domain knowledge on top. To be precise: this is about the physical AI components of these fields, not the whole of them. Much of health care and agriculture is biology and data, and will stay that way.

Health & care

Lab automation, surgical assist, rehabilitation and in-home care robotics. The hardest constraints in the stack live here: safety cases, regulatory validation, and machines that work reliably beside a person rather than behind a fence. Salem Health anchors a regional care economy and an aging Oregon makes the demand real.

Mobility

Autonomy, fleet operations, last-mile goods movement. Shares perception and safety validation almost entirely with the others, and adds the public-realm problem of machines operating where people walk. Salem's river crossings and its place on I-5 make that a practical problem here.

Built environment

Off-site fabrication, assembly robotics, building automation and sensing. Oregon's mass-timber Tech Hub and the OSU and University of Oregon TallWood Design Institute put the materials science next door. Low-volume, high-mix manufacturing is the shared problem with every other domain on this list.

The stack itself

Perception, actuation, control, sim-to-real, edge compute, power and mechanical design, plus the shop floor that turns a prototype into a hundred units. Oregon State's robotics lineage and the semiconductor cluster to the north sit on either side of it. This is the layer every other card depends on.

Agriculture

Field robotics, plant-level sensing, selective harvest, controlled-environment growing. One of the most diverse farm valleys on earth is minutes away, which makes it the rare application domain where the test environment is walking distance from the shop. OSU precision-ag research sits behind it.

The hypothesis, and the objection

Our claim: the expensive problems live at the interfaces, and teams working in isolation meet them late. The objection: application domains diverge faster than they converge, shared infrastructure is often a fiction, and proximity mostly produces meetings. We think the claim holds. We would welcome the argument.

Where To Start

Agriculture is the likeliest starting point

Of the five, agriculture is where Salem could plausibly start, because it is the one domain where the place the technology gets used is minutes from where it gets built, and it sits in one of the most agriculturally diverse regions on earth. The Willamette Valley grows more than 220 commodities; Oregon is the world's grass-seed capital and grows 99% of America's hazelnuts; nursery and greenhouse stock alone is a $1.2 billion industry. The opportunity is a hub that bridges urban innovation and the rural valley, so the technology, the processing, and the markets get built close to the people who farm.

An ag-tech testbed

Oregon State already runs precision-ag field trials on the valley's signature crops, including an AI plant-by-plant weed-control study in grass seed with the Swiss firm Ecorobotix, and has built orchard robotics for years. Oregon has strong agricultural research and few ag-tech companies, which is the gap a district could fill: somewhere to develop, prototype and demonstrate precision agriculture, controlled-environment growing and field robotics, with real fields minutes away.

From farm to market

The OSU and Oregon Department of Agriculture Food Innovation Center in Portland shows the model: shared product development, pilot processing, food safety, and market access that carry a grower's crop to a shelf. A West Salem hub can bring that closer to the farms, with shared commercial kitchens, value-added and co-packing space, and aggregation, fundable through Oregon's Resilient Food Systems Infrastructure grant.

Bridging urban and rural

The mid-Willamette Valley is home to one of the largest agricultural workforces in the country, more than 40,000 farmworkers, and to deep-rooted organizations like PCUN in Woodburn. A district that connects urban innovation to rural producers and to that workforce can spread the opportunity it creates rather than concentrate it.

Partners already next door

Chemeketa's Northwest Wine Studies Center, with an eight-acre teaching vineyard and a working estate winery, sits in West Salem itself. OSU Extension reaches every Oregon county, SEDCOR organizes the region's food and beverage economy, and the Oregon State Fair makes Salem the state's agricultural showcase. The pieces of a food and ag hub are already in the neighborhood.

The Model

Newlab, inside the Brooklyn Navy Yard

The clearest picture of what Edgewater becomes is Newlab: a venture and maker community for hard technology, housed in one rehabilitated industrial building inside a much larger working campus. It gives hardware founders three things that are hard to find alone, infrastructure to prototype, real projects to pilot, and a pathway to capital, and it sits inside a city-owned industrial yard run for local jobs. Edgewater would work the same way: a curated maker space inside a campus run for public benefit.

What Newlab is

Founded in 2016 by David Belt and Scott Cohen, modeled on the MIT Media Lab, inside Building 128, an 1899 Navy machine shop rebuilt as roughly 84,000 square feet of studios, labs, and a full fabrication shop, metal, wood, 3D printing, electronics, and CNC.

How it was built

A public landlord owned and rehabbed the shell; a private operator brought the vision and the curation. The roughly $82.8M Building 128 project drew $25M of New Markets Tax Credits alongside historic tax credits, federal, state, and city grants, and private capital. A coalition built it.

Why it grew

Shared tools, a curated membership and real pilot projects reinforce each other. Newlab reports more than 300 member startups and over 1,000 members across its locations, and has opened a second site at Michigan Central in Detroit with Ford. The model has transferred once already.

The figures we cite for Newlab and the Brooklyn Navy Yard come from those organizations and the New Markets Tax Credit Coalition. The Building 128 capital stack ($25M of New Markets Tax Credits within a roughly $82.8M project) is documented; member counts are as the organizations report them.
How These Districts Are Built

The four partners

The same structure appears in most of these districts. Four partners come together, a university, a government, an industry and a community, and a neutral entity holds the mission so that no single party owns it. This is the pattern usually called the triple helix, with the community as a fourth strand.

Each partner brings what the others cannot: the university its research and talent, government its land and patient capital, industry its anchor demand and jobs, and the community its workforce and its stake in the result. The case studies below show it.

Case Studies

Coalitions that built districts

These places began the same way Edgewater begins: a champion convened partners, a neutral entity was formed, an anchor and a public tool arrived together, and a community was kept close. Their industries range from semiconductors to biotech to clean energy; what Edgewater borrows is their method, the way a coalition turns one building into a district, which Salem applies to its own mix of fields. The closest match to West Salem is the first one.

Closest to us · St. Louis, USA

Cortex Innovation Community

Five anchor institutions, Washington University, BJC HealthCare, Saint Louis University, the University of Missouri-St. Louis, and the Missouri Botanical Garden, came together in 2002 after a university chancellor visited Kendall Square and brought his peers along to see it. They pooled roughly $29 million, formed a single nonprofit master developer, and paired it with Missouri's redevelopment and tax-increment financing powers to transform a brownfield into a district. The lesson for Salem is direct: a handful of anchors, modest pooled equity, one neutral nonprofit, and a public financing tool are enough to begin.

Brainport

A mayor, a university president, and a chamber-of-commerce president convened a standing partnership and held it in the Brainport Foundation. The region became home to ASML, one of the world's most valuable technology companies.

And across the United States

The American model began at Research Triangle Park: three universities, a governor, and business leaders placing a district in a neutral nonprofit so rival schools could cooperate and the work could move at private speed. The cases that follow refine the same idea, from a university acting like an owner to a mission nonprofit paired with a specialist developer.

Where We Begin

The first five moves

The precedents agree on the sequence. Start with a credible champion, see a working district together, form the neutral trust, land the anchor and the public tool as a pair, and prove it with one building. Each step makes the next one easier.

Move 1
Convene a champion

Bring an Oregon State and a Willamette leader to co-convene, so the effort reads as a research and civic initiative from day one.

Move 2
See it together

Take the City, the universities, the State, and an anchor employer to a working district, Newlab and Cortex, to align on one shared model.

Move 3
Form the trust

Charter a neutral district nonprofit that all can join and the community sits on, alongside Now City as the developer.

Move 4
Pair anchor & tool

Land an anchor presence in the first adaptive-reuse building together with the Riverfront-Downtown urban-renewal financing.

Move 5
Build the proof

Open the first building with its founding members, which is what makes the next commitments easier to get.

The Approach · Built to Suit

Adaptive reuse first

The district grows building by building. It starts by adapting West Salem's existing industrial buildings, raw high-bay structures rebuilt to suit makers, ventures, and labs, and adds purpose-built facilities as demand grows. Reusing existing buildings is faster, lower-carbon and cheaper to finance, and new construction follows demand rather than preceding it. The district can open early and grow on real occupancy.

Why start with existing buildings

Newlab, Michigan Central and Greentown Labs all began in old industrial buildings. Rehabilitation is also cheaper to finance: an industrial retrofit can draw historic and New Markets tax credits, as Newlab's Building 128 did. West Salem has the same building stock, and new facilities can go up beside it.

Testing in a working neighborhood

Because the workspace sits inside a mixed-use neighborhood, companies can test on streets, in homes, and across energy, water, food and mobility systems that real residents use. Detroit filled its first building within months of opening a designated testing zone. Edgewater could offer the same: somewhere to deploy as well as prototype.

What We Will Count

The outcomes we would be judged on

A district can post a strong headline while local incomes, ownership and opportunity stand still. We would rather name the outcomes now, while they can still be argued with, than claim a projection we cannot support. The precedent numbers below are what comparable districts actually returned. They are evidence that this can work, not a forecast of what this will do.

Talent kept home

Oregon educates engineers, researchers, and makers at Oregon State, Willamette, and Chemeketa, then watches many leave for bigger metros. A district gives them a reason to build their careers, and their companies, here, and a reason for those who left to return.

Founders and small business

Shared fabrication, labs, and pilot space lower the cost and risk of starting up, so more local founders, makers, and food and ag entrepreneurs can take the first step, and the second, without leaving the valley to do it.

A whole-valley economy

The district connects urban innovation to the rural Willamette: field robotics, food processing, and market access that carry the gains beyond the city, to the farmers and the workforce who already feed the region and the world.

Documented precedent, for scale rather than projection: Cortex in St. Louis grew 406 companies from a single brownfield and returned $163.8M of public revenue between 2014 and 2025. Enrico Moretti's work in The New Geography of Jobs puts the local multiplier on a traded-sector innovation job at roughly five. What Edgewater produces depends on partners, sector focus, and pace, and we will not know until it is built. The indicators below are how we propose to be judged.
The Test

Twelve indicators we would track

Most places measure progress by regional GDP, population growth, or the size of the company they recruited. Now City advisor Michael Shuman, an economist and the author of The Local Economy Solution, argues those numbers mislead, because a district can post a big headline while local incomes, ownership, and opportunity stand still. A community grows stronger when its own economy gets healthier. These are the twelve signs Shuman recommends watching, and the ones this district is built to move.

1
Median income

Whether the typical resident earns more, a truer gauge than averages a handful of high earners can inflate.

2
Inequality

Whether the gap between richest and poorest, the Gini measure, is narrowing over five to ten years.

3
Local-business jobs

The share of work in small, locally owned firms, plus public and self-employed workers, who are almost all local.

4
Economic self-reliance

How much of what the community needs it makes and buys locally instead of importing from elsewhere.

5
Small-business survival

Whether the smallest firms keep surviving and multiplying, the sign of a healthy entrepreneurial ecosystem.

6
B-Corps and benefit corps

The count of firms certified for serving workers, community, and environment, not investors alone.

7
Business-network size

How many firms join local alliances for shared marketing, purchasing, contracting, and advocacy.

8
Self-funded networks

Whether those alliances sustain themselves on member dues and services rather than grants alone.

9
Local procurement

The share of public spending that flows to local businesses, and the income multiplier it sets off.

10
Community investment

Capital raised locally through investment crowdfunding and community-backed local funds.

11
Fewer giveaways

Less public money spent luring large outside companies with grants and tax breaks.

12
Talent retained

Whether young, talented people stay and return, which attainable housing makes possible.

This district is designed to move exactly these numbers: local founders and small firms, talent kept in the valley, public dollars and investment that circulate at home, and an economy more self-reliant than the day it began.
Framework by economist and author Michael Shuman, a Now City advisor, drawn from his 2025 report card on the local-economy movement. Paraphrased here; the full method appears in his book The Local Economy Solution.
