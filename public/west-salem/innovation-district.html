District Plan · Innovation District

Oregon as a hub
for physical AI

Robotics, farming, health care, mobility and the built environment run on the same engineering. Oregon has all of it, spread thinly along one corridor.

Working notes · West Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Premise

What the technology is for

None of this technology exists to reward investors, though it will. It exists to get us faster to communities that are resilient, regenerative and worth living in. That is the test we would like to be held to, and it is why we are building a neighborhood rather than a campus. The only way to know whether machines that act on the physical world improve daily life is to put them where people live, and then measure.

Underneath sits a technical argument. Robotics, agriculture, care, mobility and the built environment are treated as five industries. From the engineering side they are five uses of one stack: perception, actuation, control, safety validation, and manufacturing in the awkward middle band. Oregon has depth in every part of it, spread across a semiconductor cluster, a robotics lineage at Oregon State, one of the most diverse farm valleys on earth, a health system and a mass-timber sector.

The hypothesis is that a great deal of value is stranded between those five, because each pays separately to build the same foundations. Put them next to each other and the shared work gets done once. What follows is the evidence from districts that worked, the evidence from those that disappointed, and what we still do not know. Every section below opens with the claim it is making, so the argument can be read in about ninety seconds and the detail taken only where it is wanted.

Why
Oregon holds the components of the physical AI stack across a semiconductor cluster, a robotics research lineage, a working farm valley and a health system. What it lacks is one place where they meet.
What
The same engineering applied to five domains, with shared fabrication and instrumentation as the common infrastructure, in the spirit of Newlab at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, set inside a West Salem neighborhood where the work can also be deployed.
How
The proven coalition playbook: a university, government, industry, and community, held by a neutral trust, anchored by adaptive reuse and urban-renewal financing.
When
A clear five-move sequence: convene a champion, see a working district together, form the trust, land the anchor and the public tool, then build the first proof.
Measure
We do not know yet. What we will commit to is what gets counted: companies still here in year five, engineers who choose to stay, and whether local incomes, ownership and small-business survival actually move.
Why Here

What Oregon already has

Oregon already has every part of the stack. What it lacks is one place where they meet. The Silicon Forest runs the length of the Willamette, from Intel and the semiconductor cluster around Portland through instrumentation, robotics, materials and research talent all the way to Eugene. It is one of the deepest technology ecosystems in the country, and Salem sits in the middle of it. What the valley does not have is a single place where these industries work near each other.

The Argument

The shared engineering

Most innovation districts pick one industry. The argument here is that these five are five uses of the same engineering. The stack underneath them is perception, actuation, control, sim-to-real, safety validation, edge compute, power and mechanical design, and a shop floor built for the volumes these products actually run at. A weeding robot, a care assistant, an autonomous shuttle and a retrofit arm share almost all of it. What differs is the domain knowledge on top, and the instrumentation the stack needs costs more than any one company can justify while sitting idle most of the week. To be precise: this is about the physical AI components of these fields rather than the whole of them. Much of health care and agriculture is biology and data, and will stay that way.

Health & care

Lab automation, surgical assist, rehabilitation and in-home care robotics. The hardest constraints in the stack live here: safety cases, regulatory validation, and machines that work reliably beside a person rather than behind a fence. Salem Health anchors a regional care economy, an aging Oregon makes the demand real, and the proposed Women & Family Health Institute would give this domain a clinical setting to be tested in.

Mobility

Autonomy, fleet operations, last-mile goods movement. Shares perception and safety validation almost entirely with the others, and adds the public-realm problem of machines operating where people walk. Salem's river crossings and its place on I-5 make that a practical problem here rather than a hypothetical one. Our own work on it runs from an autonomous aerial crossing, Whoosh, through the wider mobility concepts and the rail corridor.

The built environment

Buildings and district systems across their whole life, not the building of them. Off-site fabrication and assembly robotics at the start, then sensing, energy and water management, predictive maintenance, and the robotic retrofit and disassembly that decide whether a building is adapted or demolished. A district takes in energy, water, materials and people and handles that well or badly for eighty years. Construction is a couple of those years. Our own ambition sits here: to build homes at the scale and pace America's largest rental homebuilders manage, which is a manufacturing problem before it is an architectural one. Oregon's mass-timber Tech Hub and the TallWood Design Institute put the materials science next door.

Industry and logistics

Warehouse and materials handling, inspection, assembly, and the work people should not have to do by hand. This is the domain furthest along commercially, which makes it the one that funds the others, and Salem already has both the manufacturing and the distribution volume for it. Oregon State's robotics lineage and the semiconductor cluster to the north sit on either side of the supply chain it depends on.

Agriculture

Field robotics, plant-level sensing, selective harvest, controlled-environment growing. The Willamette Valley grows more than 220 commodities across roughly 40,000 farms, and Oregon State already runs precision-agriculture trials here, including plant-by-plant weed control in grass seed. The test environment is minutes from the workbench, which matters more in this domain than any other: a field robot that works in a laboratory tells you almost nothing about whether it works in a field.

What is locked up, and the objection

Our claim: five industries are each paying to build the same things. Perception and safety-validation methods, actuator and sensor supply, manufacturing capacity in the band described above, and somewhere to test. Built once and shared, that becomes cheaper validation, a supplier base with enough combined volume to be worth serving, engineers who can move between applications without starting over, and problems solved for all five the first time. The objection: domains diverge faster than they converge, shared infrastructure is often a fiction, and proximity mostly produces meetings. We think the value is real and currently stranded. We would welcome the argument.

The awkward middle band

These products all sit in the same manufacturing regime, and it is a genuinely difficult one. Humanoid robots are being built toward ten thousand units a year. Field robots, care devices and shuttles run in the thousands. Industrialized housing components run in the tens of thousands. That is far too many to hand-build, and far too few to justify the hard tooling and dedicated lines that make a car cheap.

The consequence is a supplier problem. At those volumes a component maker will not retool for you, quote you a serious price, or take your call before the automotive customer's. Every company in this band pays more, waits longer and carries inventory it should not have to. One firm at that volume is a nuisance to a supplier. Five industries at that volume, in one place, is a customer.

It is also where our own ambition lives. Building housing at the scale and pace of America's largest rental homebuilders means moving construction into this band and then out the other side of it, using flexible automation rather than craft labor. The engineering problem is the same one the robotics companies are solving, which is the most concrete reason we think these belong in one place.

The Scale Argument

We think the district itself is physical AI, built at the size where people live and work

A robot is a body that senses, decides and acts on the physical world. A district is the same thing at another scale. Streets, buildings, energy, water and movement systems that sense, respond, and shape what happens to the people inside them. We do not think of the neighborhood as a testbed for physical AI. We think it is physical AI, built at the size where people live.

A district is a metabolism

It takes in energy, water, materials, food and people, moves them around, and puts something back out, every day for decades. Physical AI and the software above it will eventually touch all of that: how a building conditions itself, how water is cleaned and reused, how goods arrive, how a street is maintained. Treating the district as one system rather than a set of separate assets is the difference between a neighborhood that improves with age and one that decays on schedule.

Validated only in use

A machine can be validated in a laboratory. A district can only be validated in use, over years, by the people living in it, which makes it the slowest and most honest instrument in the stack. Companies working here can test on real streets, in real homes, and across the energy, water, food and mobility systems residents depend on. Detroit filled its first building within months of designating a testing zone.

Built to be experimented in

Walk the Brooklyn Navy Yard and you meet autonomous shuttles moving between buildings. It is genuinely good fun, and it is the sort of thing that makes a person want to live there. What that campus lacks is homes, so nobody can. A district with designated testing zones, residents who opted in, and published results gets to be both: a place where new things are tried in public, and a place people live.

The Human Question

How we would know it worked for people

Every application on this page ends in a human body: a farmworker's back, a nurse's shift, a person recovering after surgery, a family walking home. A district built on physical AI needs somewhere serious to ask whether those outcomes actually improved, and to publish the answer when they did not.

The proposed Women & Family Health Institute is a working clinic that doubles as a research institute, founded around one question: what would it take for a woman to thrive across her whole life, and to raise children who thrive. It is deliberately small, it is led by clinicians rather than by a lab, and it publishes openly, including what fails.

It sits in this page for two reasons. Health and care is the hardest application of physical AI, because a machine working beside a person carries a safety case that a machine behind a fence does not, and that work needs a clinical setting to be evaluated in. And a district designed as a physical system needs an instrument pointed at the humans inside it, or it is only measuring itself.

What it contributes to the district

A clinical partner for care robotics, rehabilitation and monitoring work. A research agenda set by what families report rather than by what is fundable. And a standing check on whether the neighborhood is delivering the outcomes it was built for.

What the district contributes to it

Homes families can afford, early learning close to daily life, a riverfront to grow up beside, and workspace a short walk from the clinic, so what the research proves has somewhere to be built.

The institute is a working concept in early development. No institute, clinic or fund has been formed, and the founding roles are described by function rather than by name. Read the concept.
The Neighborhood

Designed to attract and retain talent

Recruiting is the constraint most hardware companies in this valley name first. An engineer choosing between Salem and a larger metro is not comparing job offers alone, they are comparing lives, and a partner's job prospects, rent, and what there is to do on a Saturday decide as many moves as salary does. So the district is planned as a neighborhood that happens to contain workspace, rather than workspace with housing attached.

Homes, priced against local salaries

Roughly a thousand homes are planned in the district, in Scandinavian-style courtyard blocks: studios through family flats, rental and ownership, with a share held permanently attainable through shared equity so the next family in can still afford the block. The mix is designed against what people here actually earn.

Live-work and maker lofts

The bow-truss campus pairs high-bay workshop space with lofts above it, for the founder who is also the first employee and the maker whose rent has to cover both a home and a bench. It is the cheapest space in the district and the first thing being brought back into use.

A car-light daily life

Short blocks, a market hall, a riverfront, and a crossing to downtown, planned so a household can run on one car or none. The saving is not incidental. For an early-career engineer it is the difference between affording to live here and not.

Somewhere to be on a Saturday

A daily market, an arts district in the existing buildings, food and small retail at ground level, and a restored river edge to walk, row and swim from. Activation starts before the permanent buildings, because the neighborhood has to be worth visiting before it is worth moving to.

Family infrastructure

Early learning inside the blocks rather than across town, and a clinical partner in the health institute concept. The thing that most often decides whether a hire stays past year three is whether their family is settled.

Built for the long term

Mass timber, Passive House performance, district energy, and water treated as a resource. Lower operating costs for residents, and a place that still works in the climate the next forty years will bring.

The longer versions: the District Vision for the plan as a whole, Placemaking for what daily life is designed to feel like, Activation for the market, arts district and live-work campus, Mobility for the crossings, and Edgewater 2036.

If you would rather read it as a life than as a plan, Edgewater 2036 is a written scenario of one family's ordinary Tuesday here ten years from now: a robotics engineer, a designer who founded a company, two small children, and the grandparents four hundred meters away. It is fiction and says so. It is also the fastest way to judge whether this is a place the people you are trying to hire would actually choose.

The Hub Model

Newlab is the model for one building

Newlab is the clearest model we have found for the workspace at the center of a district: a venture and maker community for hard technology, in one rehabilitated industrial building, giving founders infrastructure to prototype, real projects to pilot and a path to capital. It is a hub, and we would build one. It is worth being precise about what it is not. Newlab is a single building inside a gated industrial campus with no homes, no streets of its own and nothing open in the evening. The hub is the part we borrow. The neighborhood around it is the part we add, and it is the part most of this page is about.

What Newlab is

Founded in 2016 by David Belt and Scott Cohen, modeled on the MIT Media Lab, inside Building 128, an 1899 Navy machine shop rebuilt as roughly 84,000 square feet of studios, labs, and a full fabrication shop, metal, wood, 3D printing, electronics, and CNC.

How it was built

A public landlord owned and rehabbed the shell; a private operator brought the vision and the curation. The roughly $82.8M Building 128 project drew $25M of New Markets Tax Credits alongside historic tax credits, federal, state, and city grants, and private capital. A coalition built it.

Why the hub model grew

Shared tools, a curated membership and real pilot projects reinforce each other. Newlab reports more than 300 member startups and over 1,000 members across its locations, and has opened a second site at Michigan Central in Detroit with Ford. The model has transferred once already.

The figures we cite for Newlab and the Brooklyn Navy Yard come from those organizations and the New Markets Tax Credit Coalition. The Building 128 capital stack ($25M of New Markets Tax Credits within a roughly $82.8M project) is documented; member counts are as the organizations report them.
How These Districts Are Built

The four partners

The same structure appears in most of these districts. Four partners come together, a university, a government, an industry and a community, and a neutral entity holds the mission so that no single party owns it. This is the pattern usually called the triple helix, with the community as a fourth strand.

Each partner brings what the others cannot: the university its research and talent, government its land and patient capital, industry its anchor demand and jobs, and the community its workforce and its stake in the result. The case studies below show it.

Case Studies

Coalitions that built districts

These places began the same way Edgewater begins: a champion convened partners, a neutral entity was formed, an anchor and a public tool arrived together, and a community was kept close. Their industries range from semiconductors to biotech to clean energy; what Edgewater borrows is their method, the way a coalition turns one building into a district, which Salem applies to its own mix of fields. The closest match to West Salem is the first one.

Closest to us · St. Louis, USA

Cortex Innovation Community

Five anchor institutions, Washington University, BJC HealthCare, Saint Louis University, the University of Missouri-St. Louis, and the Missouri Botanical Garden, came together in 2002 after a university chancellor visited Kendall Square and brought his peers along to see it. They pooled roughly $29 million, formed a single nonprofit master developer, and paired it with Missouri's redevelopment and tax-increment financing powers to transform a brownfield into a district. The lesson for Salem is direct: a handful of anchors, modest pooled equity, one neutral nonprofit, and a public financing tool are enough to begin.

A correction we owe this page. An earlier version of this table claimed almost none of these districts had homes. That was wrong, and the real picture is more useful. Brookings, in the 2014 report that gave the term its meaning, defines an innovation district as offering mixed-use housing alongside the work. So housing is not our invention. It is the orthodoxy, unevenly applied.
What the record actually shows, in three tiers. A minority built housing in from the start: Singapore's One-North had residential in the 2001 masterplan; Barcelona's 2000 rezoning legally required 4,000 subsidized homes as the price of the innovation land use; Mission Rock in San Francisco is 40% affordable by design; Cambridge's Eddington and Oxford's Begbroke pair thousands of homes with science-park expansion. A larger group added housing later, usually under outside pressure: Adlershof now has around 3,900 homes and 6,000 residents, Cortex broke ground on its first 174 apartments in 2026 after two decades, and Kendall Square is only now filling its Volpe site with roughly 1,400 units following a city rezoning. And a small famous group still has none: the Brooklyn Navy Yard by industrial zoning, and Research Triangle Park by a covenant that stood for sixty-five years until it was repealed in November 2025.
Two honest caveats. Barcelona's 4,000 units were a requirement, not a delivery; roughly 1,581 are built or under construction twenty-five years on. And the peer-reviewed critique of this field is not that housing is missing but that the housing which gets built skews expensive, so the people already living nearby are pushed out. That is the failure we are trying to design against, and it is the reason shared equity is in the plan rather than a note at the end of it.

The Hardest Seat

What an anchor actually is

In every district we studied, one commitment came first and made the rest possible. The anchors were not all the same kind of organization, and the difference is worth understanding before deciding who to ask.

A health system

At Cortex, hospital and university anchors pooled roughly $29M and formed a nonprofit master developer. A health system has long-horizon capital, a permanent local presence, and a research mission. In Salem the regional care economy is the obvious place to look, and the health institute concept is one route to that conversation.

A research institute placed on site

Berlin moved university science faculties physically to Adlershof. Singapore moved national research institutes into One-North before the private tenants arrived. A relocated lab is the strongest possible signal, and the most expensive to arrange.

A dominant regional employer

Eindhoven organized around one company's supply chain and its people. This is the fastest route and the most fragile, because the district's fortunes track a single firm. Salem has candidates in advanced manufacturing and logistics; none has been approached about this.

A public revenue engine

Medellin funded Ruta N from a publicly owned utility's dividends and seated citizens as a full partner. The anchor need not be a tenant at all. It can be a durable source of money with a public mandate attached.

Named institutions above are where the conversation would start, based on what each already does. None of them has committed to anything described on this page, and we would expect the eventual list to look different.
Where We Begin

The first five moves

The precedents agree on the sequence. Start with a credible champion, see a working district together, form the neutral trust, land the anchor and the public tool as a pair, and prove it with one building. Each step makes the next one easier.

Move 1
Convene a champion

Bring an Oregon State and a Willamette leader to co-convene, so the effort reads as a research and civic initiative from day one.

Move 2
See it together

Take the City, the universities, the State, and an anchor employer to a working district, Newlab and Cortex, to align on one shared model.

Move 3
Form the trust

Charter a neutral district nonprofit that all can join and the community sits on, alongside Now City as the developer.

Move 4
Pair anchor & tool

Land an anchor presence in the first adaptive-reuse building together with the Riverfront-Downtown urban-renewal financing.

Move 5
Build the proof

Open the first building with its founding members, which is what makes the next commitments easier to get.

The Approach · Built to Suit

Adaptive reuse first

Start with the buildings that are already standing. The district grows building by building. It starts by adapting West Salem's existing industrial buildings, raw high-bay structures rebuilt to suit makers, ventures, and labs, and adds purpose-built facilities as demand grows. Reusing existing buildings is faster, lower-carbon and cheaper to finance, and new construction follows demand rather than preceding it. The district can open early and grow on real occupancy.

Why start with existing buildings

Newlab, Michigan Central and Greentown Labs all began in old industrial buildings. Rehabilitation is also cheaper to finance: an industrial retrofit can draw historic and New Markets tax credits, as Newlab's Building 128 did. West Salem has the same building stock, and new facilities can go up beside it.

What gets built new

Purpose-built shared facilities follow demand: fabrication and instrumentation that no single company would buy alone, and the labs and high-bay space the applications on this page require. The order matters. Retrofit proves the occupancy that justifies the new construction.

What We Will Count

The outcomes we would be judged on

A district can post a strong headline while local incomes, ownership and opportunity stand still. We would rather name the outcomes now, while they can still be argued with, than claim a projection we cannot support. Two things need counting, and they are usually treated as opposites.

Companies that come and stay

Firms that move a team here and then expand it. Companies started here that reach their second and third building without leaving. Suppliers that follow their customers into the valley. The measure is not the announcement, it is what is still here in year five.

Engineers who choose to stay

Oregon educates engineers and makers and then watches many leave. The harder question is the one hiring managers here already face: what would make a graduate of a serious robotics program move to Salem, and still be here in five years? Housing they can afford, a life outside work, and a partner who can also find a job are the usual answers, and all three are things a district can affect.

Local ownership and local incomes

Whether the typical household here earns more, whether small firms survive and multiply, whether capital raised locally stays local, and whether the gains reach the rural valley and its workforce rather than concentrating in the district.

Why these are complementary rather than opposed

Attracting companies and building local ownership are usually argued as a choice. The argument turns on how the companies are won. Michael Shuman, an advisor here, is direct that public money spent luring firms with grants and tax breaks tends to subtract from a local economy rather than add to it, and one of his twelve indicators counts exactly that. What he is objecting to is the cheque, not the company.

Competing on the place is a different instrument. Homes people can afford, shared fabrication no single firm would buy alone, a neighborhood an engineer wants to live in, and a workforce pipeline that already exists. Those are assets the community keeps whether or not any particular company says yes, and they build local capacity while they attract outside investment. That is the version of attraction we are trying to test, and it is the only version we think survives Shuman's scorecard.

Documented precedent, for scale rather than projection: Cortex in St. Louis grew 406 companies from a single brownfield and returned $163.8M of public revenue between 2014 and 2025. Enrico Moretti's work in The New Geography of Jobs puts the local multiplier on a traded-sector innovation job at roughly five. What Edgewater produces depends on partners, sector focus and pace, and we will not know until it is built.
The Test

Twelve indicators we would track

Most places measure progress by regional GDP, population growth, or the size of the company they recruited. Now City advisor Michael Shuman, an economist and the author of The Local Economy Solution, argues those numbers mislead, because a district can post a big headline while local incomes, ownership, and opportunity stand still. A community grows stronger when its own economy gets healthier. These are the twelve signs Shuman recommends watching, and the ones this district is built to move.

1
Median income

Whether the typical resident earns more, a truer gauge than averages a handful of high earners can inflate.

2
Inequality

Whether the gap between richest and poorest, the Gini measure, is narrowing over five to ten years.

3
Local-business jobs

The share of work in small, locally owned firms, plus public and self-employed workers, who are almost all local.

4
Economic self-reliance

How much of what the community needs it makes and buys locally instead of importing from elsewhere.

5
Small-business survival

Whether the smallest firms keep surviving and multiplying, the sign of a healthy entrepreneurial ecosystem.

6
B-Corps and benefit corps

The count of firms certified for serving workers, community, and environment, not investors alone.

7
Business-network size

How many firms join local alliances for shared marketing, purchasing, contracting, and advocacy.

8
Self-funded networks

Whether those alliances sustain themselves on member dues and services rather than grants alone.

9
Local procurement

The share of public spending that flows to local businesses, and the income multiplier it sets off.

10
Community investment

Capital raised locally through investment crowdfunding and community-backed local funds.

11
Fewer giveaways

Less public money spent luring large outside companies with grants and tax breaks.

12
Talent retained

Whether young, talented people stay and return, which attainable housing makes possible.

This district is designed to move exactly these numbers: local founders and small firms, talent kept in the valley, public dollars and investment that circulate at home, and an economy more self-reliant than the day it began.

The Feasibility Work

What it would take to test any of this

Nothing on this page should be believed until it has been studied by people with no stake in the answer. Three pieces of work would test it, and they are worth funding whichever way they come out. If the argument holds, it is the basis for a district and a great deal of housing. If it fails, Salem learns cheaply why, and so does everyone else working on the same problem.

1. Market and workforce

Who lives where now, what share of the advanced-industry workforce commutes in, what would move them, and what housing types and rents the corridor supports across income bands. The one piece we have actually priced.

2. Site and district feasibility

Both banks of the river: title, environmental, geotechnical, utilities, stormwater, transportation and entitlement pathway, then a concept framework and a financeable first phase. Findings sorted plainly into no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or potential fatal flaw.

3. Mid-volume manufacturing

Whether a manufacturing base serving the corridor holds up: aggregated demand, transport economics for components rather than modules, the state code pathway, a financing instrument that fits factory production, and honest cost against site-built. Set out in the Oregon homes strategy. Unpriced, because we have found nobody who has done it.

How the jobs number would be built

The first question anyone asks is how many jobs, and we would rather show the method than assert a figure. A number produced before the study is a number produced by whoever wants it to be large. So the economic and fiscal impact workstream above has a specification rather than a target.

Direct employment, from space and density

Square feet delivered by phase, multiplied by employment density for the actual use. Roughly 250 square feet per worker for office, 400 to 500 for laboratory, and 500 to 800 for maker and light industrial space. This is arithmetic rather than forecasting, and it is only as good as the absorption assumption underneath it.

Total employment, from published multipliers

Direct jobs run through regional input-output multipliers for the specific Oregon sector codes, using the Bureau of Economic Analysis RIMS II tables or IMPLAN. For traded-sector manufacturing these typically land somewhere between two and two and a half total jobs per direct job. Higher figures circulate in this field and should be treated with suspicion.

A recruitment ladder, not a single case

Three scenarios with named trigger conditions: slow, where space fills at regional averages; expected, where an anchor commits and pulls suppliers; and strong, where several employers arrive together. Each carries its own absorption curve, and the spread between them is more informative than any midpoint.

Two honesty tests we would insist on

Construction employment counted separately and in person-years, because it is temporary and mixing it into a headline inflates everything. And for the manufacturing question, net rather than gross: factory work substitutes for some site labor, so the study has to say what is genuinely additional and what has moved.

What a comparable actually produced. Cortex in St. Louis reports 5,400 people employed and 406 companies on a 200-acre former brownfield, roughly two decades after five anchor institutions pooled about $29 million. That is the honest shape of the opportunity: real, large, and slow. Any figure we published today would be a scaled guess against that, which is why the study has to produce it instead.

Who could hold the money

Now City cannot be the applicant for most of this, and that shapes everything. Federal and state programs will not pay a private developer to study its own site. The eligible applicant is a city, a county, an economic development district, a university, a tribe, or a nonprofit acting with a local government. So the first task is sponsor recruitment, not grant writing.

The good news is that the host does not have to be a charity. EDA's rules make eligible any public or private nonprofit acting in cooperation with a political subdivision, with no 501(c)(3) requirement, which puts regional economic development organizations squarely in scope. SEDCOR, the Mid-Willamette Valley's economic development organization, is the obvious candidate to talk to first, and it has done both halves of this before: it held an EDA Build to Scale award for the Northwest Ag Innovation Hub, and it convened the Newberg workforce housing consortium that secured a $3M state allocation in 2022 and has administered it as a fund ever since. That is a model to learn from rather than reinvent, and whether SEDCOR wants the role is their decision to make, not ours to assume.

Two eligibility limits worth naming early. EPA Brownfields assessment grants were opened to nonprofits by the 2018 BUILD Act, but only to 501(c)(3)s, so that application needs a charity or a public body as applicant rather than a trade association. And Oregon's transportation and growth management grants exclude nonprofits entirely; only cities, counties, tribes, councils of government and special districts may apply.

And a practical one that shapes the ask. Federal awards reimburse after money is spent, so any host carries the cost until it is repaid. For a lean organization that is real exposure, which is why an advance structure or a state appropriation is usually the workable route, and why the ask should be sized to what a host can comfortably carry rather than to what we would like to spend.

Two rules we would hold to whoever hosts it. Describe consultant roles rather than naming firms before an award, because federal procurement standards govern how a public grantee selects its team. And check pre-award cost rules before engaging anyone, because most programs will not reimburse work that started before the award landed.

The doors that are actually open

Verified against program sources in August and September 2026. Dates move, so each is worth confirming before it is relied on.

West Salem URANow · City applies
The city's own urban renewal opportunity fund runs to roughly $50,000 to $100,000 a year and sits on the exact corridor. Smallest paperwork, fastest answer, and the relationship matters more than the money.
EDA planning and technical assistanceSpring 2027 · A regional partner applies
Feasibility studies are the textbook use of this program, and the applicant does not need to be a charity. The prerequisite is getting the project onto the region's comprehensive economic development strategy list, which is slow and should start regardless of anything else.
EPA BrownfieldsAutumn 2026 · City or a 501(c)(3)
Community-wide assessment grants fund reuse planning as well as site assessment, and the riverfront industrial legacy is the hook. Note the charity requirement, which narrows who can hold this one.
Oregon TGM planning grants2027 cycle · City only
Integrated land use and transportation planning, delivered as consultant services rather than cash. Cities choose their candidates months ahead, so the work is getting into Salem's internal queue this autumn.
Business Oregon and Regional SolutionsRolling · A door, not a program
The state's regional coordination function convenes agencies around one project and finds the money that fits. Eligibility varies by program, so this is a conversation before it is an application.
The 2027 legislative sessionGroundwork now
A member-carried funding request is a real lane in Oregon and it is the one that has already worked here: the $3M that funded the Newberg consortium came this way in 2022. It runs on relationships and session timing rather than a deadline, so it starts a year ahead.
PhilanthropyNeeds a charity applicant
Oregon's statewide and family foundations fund predevelopment and planning readily, but the recipient has to be a 501(c)(3). Until an established one is involved, a fiscal sponsor is the interim route.
And an honest limit. Grant timelines can outrun a project. If nothing is executable within a reasonable window we would fund the market study ourselves and proceed, because a study that arrives two years late has answered a question nobody is asking any more.
