Working Strategy · Oregon

Oregon as a housing
manufacturing base
for the West Coast

The West Coast is short roughly 245,000 homes a year. The last generation of factory homebuilders raised billions and mostly went bankrupt. What did they run into, and is there anything a state can do about it that a company cannot? Oregon would be where this is based. The corridor is the market.

Working notes · West Salem, Oregon · September 2026
The Gap

What the three states say they need, against what they build

Demand is not the problem here and never has been. Every West Coast state now publishes a legal housing production target, and every one of them is missing it by a wide margin. These are the states' own numbers, not ours.

Mid-Volume Manufacturing

Making things in the thousands

Homes, robots, farm equipment, shuttles and medical devices share a manufacturing problem, and it is not the one people assume. They are not made by hand, and they are not made by the million. They are made in the thousands and the tens of thousands, which is the hardest volume in manufacturing to serve.

Below this band

Craft production. A few hundred units a year, made largely by hand, priced accordingly. Most of American homebuilding sits here, one house at a time, which is why the cost per home has barely moved in decades.

Mid-volume, the band in question

Thousands to tens of thousands of complex units a year. Too many to build by hand. Too few to justify the dedicated tooling and hard automation that make a car cheap. Humanoid robots, field robots, building systems and shuttles all sit here.

Above this band

Mass production. Hundreds of thousands or millions of identical units, where fixed tooling costs disappear into volume and suppliers compete for the work. Cars, appliances, consumer electronics.

The consequence is a supplier problem, and it is the clearest reason to put these industries in one place. At mid-volume a component maker will not retool for you, will not quote you a serious price, and will not take your call before the automotive customer's. Every company in this band pays more, waits longer and carries inventory it should not have to. One firm at that volume is a nuisance to a supplier. Several industries at that volume, in one region, is a customer.

The Record

The last generation raised billions and mostly failed

Any serious version of this argument has to start with the graveyard, because the graveyard is recent and it is large. We would rather put it at the top of our own page than have someone else put it in front of us.

Katerra

Raised about $2 billion, largely from SoftBank, at a $4 billion valuation. Bankrupt in June 2021. It tried to integrate design, manufacturing and general contracting at once, before any one of them worked.

Veev

Raised several hundred million and reached unicorn valuation in 2022. Shut down in November 2023 when a funding round fell through. Its assets went to Lennar in a fire sale.

Entekra

Backed by Louisiana-Pacific with a $35M factory rated for 3,000 house frames a year. Closed in 2023, unable to reach the volume the plant needed.

Factory_OS

California's flagship modular plant at Mare Island, restructured and renamed in 2024. Filed a notice in February 2026 threatening to lay off all 280 employees.

Connect Homes

Closed its only California factory in March 2024 and moved to Arizona, leaving customers mid-order.

S2A Modular

Raised roughly $65 million from around 350 retail investors for factories that mostly went unbuilt. Its founders were sued by the SEC in July 2026.

None of these were demand failures. The waiting list was never the problem. They failed against the five constraints in the next section, and those constraints are structural rather than managerial, which is why more capital made the failures faster rather than smaller.
The Constraints

Five problems, and who can actually fix each

Each constraint has a company answer that has been tried, and a state answer that has not been tried here. The company answers are why the graveyard exists. Whether the state answers work is the open question, and it is the reason we think a region rather than a firm might be the right unit to attack this with.

Constraint 

The company answer, already tried

The state answer, not yet tried here

Factory utilizationThe killer

A plant needs 70 to 80 percent throughput to break even, and construction demand is lumpy, seasonal and rate-sensitive. Every failure above built capacity ahead of committed orders.

Aggregate demand into a standing order book. Public housing agencies, universities, health systems, cities and several developers, pooled into predictable annual volume. This is exactly what the durable European producers have and no American company has ever had.

Transport distanceThe physics

Whole modules travel 250 to 400 miles economically. Transport runs $15 to $20 a mile before escorts, and buildings are cheap relative to their bulk. HUD found the same ceiling in the 1960s.

Ship components rather than boxes, and change the cost per mile. Flat panels load densely on standard trailers with no oversize permits. Autonomous electric freight on the existing rail line attacks the constraint itself rather than working inside it, and no single company can build that.

Construction financeThe quiet one

Sixty to eighty percent of a building's value is completed inside a factory, months before anything appears on site. Lenders release draws against visible site progress, so the manufacturer carries the work in progress.

A standing facility that lets lenders advance against factory work in progress. Credit enhancement or a state-backed off-site progress instrument. Small policy, large unlock, and it works for every manufacturer in the state rather than one deal at a time.

Code fragmentationAlready solved

A wall engineered for one state's code does not transfer to another's, and each jurisdiction has its own approval. This is the single biggest reason manufactured housing outperforms modular: it has one federal code.

Oregon already has this and does not market it. The Prefabricated Structures Program under ORS 455.705 gives one plan approval and a state insignia valid in every Oregon jurisdiction, with reciprocity agreements already in place with other states.

Capital durationThe mismatch

Venture capital wants scale in five to seven years. This business has low margins, high capital cost, a hard geographic ceiling and a payback measured in decades. Every durable success ran on patient balance sheets instead.

Pension capital is the right duration. Oregon holds one of the largest public pension funds in the country. An in-state allocation to housing production infrastructure matches a thirty-year liability to a thirty-year asset, and it is a decision only the state can make.

Sources for the constraint figures: McKinsey's 2019 work on modular construction for the utilization and savings analysis; oversize transport permit and escort rates from commercial haulers; HUD's Operation Breakthrough evaluation for the historic shipping radius; the Construction Financial Management Association on off-site draw schedules; Oregon Building Codes Division for the Prefabricated Structures Program.
The Position

What Oregon already owns and does not use

The unusual thing about this argument is how little of it requires anything new. Four of the five instruments already exist in Oregon. They have simply never been pointed at the same target.

One code
The Prefabricated Structures Program gives statewide plan approval and an Oregon insignia, plus reciprocity with other states. Oregon is structurally better ground for offsite manufacturing than California is, today, and almost nobody knows it.
A legal target
The Oregon Housing Needs Analysis sets a binding production number of 29,522 homes a year, and every city now has to plan against it. That is the demand aggregation problem already half-solved, in statute.
Patient capital
A public pension fund of national scale, with the duration this kind of asset needs and a plausible in-state mandate.
A freight corridor
An existing rail line down the middle of the state, and companies now building autonomous battery-electric rail vehicles for exactly this kind of regional freight. See the rail corridor.
Robotics depth
A robotics research lineage at Oregon State, a semiconductor cluster to the north, humanoid manufacturing already in Salem, and two federal Tech Hubs. The machines that would automate this factory are the same stack. See the innovation district.
Mass timber
A federal Tech Hub, the TallWood Design Institute, and a materials industry already in the state. The thing being manufactured can be grown here too.
The honest caveat: Oregon manufacturing employment was about 178,900 in 2025 and fell by roughly 10,000 in the year to June 2026. That is a shrinking industrial base. It is a reason to act rather than a reason not to, and it is the strongest argument in any federal application.
The Strategy

Could Oregon become the place where mid-volume works?

The goal we would propose is neither a factory nor winning one company, but removing the constraints that killed the category, so that companies in this band have a structural reason to be here. Housing is the anchor demand because it is the largest and the most urgent, but the same conditions serve robotics, farm equipment, building systems and mobility.

1. Aggregate the demand

Convene the buyers who already have obligations: housing agencies, universities, health systems, cities, and developers with pipeline. Convert scattered intentions into a multi-year order book a manufacturer can finance against. This is the move that decides whether anything else matters.

2. Make components, not boxes

Panels, cassettes and kits ship densely on standard trailers and reach the whole corridor. Whole modules do not leave the Pacific Northwest. The product decision is also the market-size decision.

3. Fix the draw schedule

Stand up an instrument that lets lenders advance against verified factory progress. Until this exists, every manufacturer in the state finances its own work in progress and most of them run out of money doing it.

4. Market the code advantage

Oregon's statewide prefabrication approval and its reciprocity agreements are a genuine competitive asset that no one is selling. Extend the reciprocity list and put it in front of every manufacturer considering a West Coast site.

5. Match the capital to the asset

Pension and insurance capital, structured for thirty years rather than seven. The failures in this sector were funded by money in a hurry, and the successes were not.

And then automate

With utilization solved and capital patient, robotic automation becomes worth its cost. The jobs move from framing crews to controls, maintenance, logistics and the building of the machines themselves, which is the same work the rest of the corridor already does.

The Test

What would have to be true

We would rather publish the conditions that would sink this than be asked for them. Each of these is checkable, and any one of them failing changes the answer.

  1. Aggregated demand has to be real rather than rhetorical. A letter of interest is not an order book. The test is whether public and institutional buyers will commit to multi-year volume, and if they will not, this is a conventional regional manufacturer serving Oregon and Washington.
  2. Component shipping economics have to hold across the corridor. Panels travel further than modules, but not infinitely. If the practical reach turns out to be Oregon and Washington only, the addressable gap is about 33,500 homes a year rather than 245,000. That is still a real business and a much smaller idea.
  3. Autonomous freight has to arrive within about five years, not fifteen. It is the one variable that has genuinely changed since the last generation failed, and it is the one that puts California inside the circle. Everything we claim about corridor scale depends on it.
  4. The state has to use two instruments it already holds. The prefabrication code advantage and a pension allocation both exist as powers today. Neither requires new legislation to explore. If neither gets used, the rest is a private company solving private problems.
  5. Cost has to actually fall. McKinsey put the ceiling at more than 20 percent savings at scale and called it the exception rather than the norm. Careful independent analysis finds little to no saving at typical volumes. We should expect no saving until utilization is proven, and treat any earlier claim as marketing.
The Pattern

What has actually worked

Everywhere this has been done durably, it shares the same three conditions. Not better technology, and not more capital. The successful producers did not out-innovate the constraints in the previous sections. They accepted them and organized around them.

01

Captive or repeating demand

A buyer who comes back every year, not a sales pipeline rebuilt from scratch each quarter.

02

Patient capital, not venture

Balance sheets that can wait, matched to an asset with a payback measured in decades.

03

Decades, not years

Thirty years of compounding, rather than a five-year plan to reach national scale.

Lindbäcks, Sweden

Around 1,500 apartments a year from a single plant, built up over thirty years, selling mostly to municipal housing companies it has known that whole time. The order book is the product of the relationships, not the marketing.

Clayton Homes, United States

Small plants a day's drive apart, one federal code across every state, and its own finance arm. It succeeds by accepting the shipping radius, the code problem and the lending problem rather than fighting any of them.

That is the posture we would propose. On the first condition we can offer a beginning rather than a solution: if this idea gets legs, Salem could support an innovation district and several thousand homes over the coming decade. That is not the standing order book Lindbäcks has. It is the first entry in one.

Where This Sits

The district is the demonstration, not the point

The district is the proving ground

Edgewater in West Salem, and potentially the east bank across the river, is where the first homes and the first shared facilities would go, and where the systems can be tested by people actually living with them. See the District Vision.

The corridor is the market

Interstate 5 and the rail line beside it run from British Columbia to the Mexican border. Salem sits within an hour of everything on the northern half and within reach of the rest if freight economics change. Oregon is where this would be based and where the enabling conditions sit. It is a mark of origin rather than a limit on who it serves.

The state is the enabling layer

Four of the five constraints can only be addressed by a state, which is why these notes are addressed to Oregon rather than to investors. The instruments are the code program, a pension allocation, aggregated public demand and the freight corridor.

The Near-Term Ask

Fund the study, not the factory

Nothing on this page is a proposal to build anything yet. What we are looking for now is grant and other early funding to test it: a feasibility study covering both banks of the river in Salem, the innovation district concepts, and whether a mid-volume manufacturing base in Oregon holds up against real numbers on demand, transport, code, finance and cost.

That study is worth funding whichever way it comes out. If the argument holds, it is the basis for a great deal of housing and a manufacturing base the state currently lacks. If it does not, Oregon learns cheaply why, and so does everyone else working on the same problem.

The Feasibility Work

What it would take to test any of this

Nothing on this page should be believed until it has been studied by people with no stake in the answer. Three pieces of work would test it, and they are worth funding whichever way they come out. If the argument holds, it is the basis for a manufacturing base the state currently lacks. If it fails, Oregon learns cheaply why, and so does everyone else working on the same problem.

1. Market and workforce

Who lives where now, what share of the advanced-industry workforce commutes in, what would move them, and what housing types and rents the corridor actually supports across income bands. Run by an independent consultant so the answer is not ours to shade. Roughly $30K to $60K.

2. Site and district feasibility

Both banks of the river: title, environmental, geotechnical, utilities, stormwater, transportation and entitlement pathway, then a concept framework and a financeable first phase. Findings sorted plainly into no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or potential fatal flaw. Roughly $150K to $250K.

3. Mid-volume manufacturing

The question this page raises, tested: whether aggregated demand can be assembled, what components actually cost to move down the corridor, whether the state code pathway and a factory-progress lending instrument work as described, and what any of it does to cost per home against site-built. See also the innovation district. Roughly $60K to $120K.

How it would be funded, and why the structure matters

Now City cannot be the applicant for most of this, and that shapes everything. Federal and state programs will not pay a private developer to study its own site. The eligible applicant is a city, a county, an economic development district, a university, a tribe, or a nonprofit acting with a local government. So the work is sponsor recruitment before it is grant writing.

Which means the scope has to be split honestly. The public-benefit half, corridor feasibility, workforce and employment, infrastructure and energy, housing demand across income bands, transportation and public realm, is work a public body can own and publish, and it is the half worth funding publicly. The proprietary half, our rents, returns and capital plan, we pay for ourselves. A study we do not fully control is still worth more than no study.

Two practical rules we would hold to. Describe consultant roles rather than naming firms before an award, because federal procurement standards govern how a public grantee selects its team. And check pre-award cost rules before engaging anyone, because most programs will not reimburse work that started before the award landed.

Total range: roughly $250,000 to $500,000, with $300,000 to $350,000 a disciplined working target. A meaningful share could come as contributed or discounted professional time from consultants who would benefit if the project proceeds, which several programs will recognize as match.

The doors that are actually open

Verified against program sources in August and September 2026. Dates move, so each is worth confirming before it is relied on.

West Salem URANow
The city's own urban renewal opportunity fund runs to roughly $50,000 to $100,000 a year and sits on the exact corridor. Smallest paperwork, fastest answer, and the relationship matters more than the money.
EPA BrownfieldsSolicitation expected this autumn
Community-wide assessment grants fund reuse planning as well as site assessment. The applicant is the City or a nonprofit acting with it, and the riverfront industrial legacy is the hook.
EDA planning and technical assistanceNext cycle spring 2027
Feasibility studies are the textbook use of this program. The applicant is the regional economic development district, and the prerequisite is getting the project onto its comprehensive economic development strategy list, which is slow and should start regardless.
Oregon TGM planning grants2027 cycle
Integrated land use and transportation planning, delivered as consultant services rather than cash. Cities apply and choose their candidates months ahead, so the work is getting into Salem's internal queue.
Business Oregon and Regional SolutionsRolling
The state's regional coordination function convenes agencies around one project and finds the money that fits. A door rather than a program, and the strongest state relationship available.
The 2027 legislative sessionGroundwork now
A member-carried funding request is a real lane in Oregon: a workforce housing consortium in Newberg secured $3M this way in 2022. It runs on relationships and session timing rather than a deadline, so it starts a year ahead.
PhilanthropyBlocked until there is a nonprofit
Oregon's statewide and family foundations fund predevelopment and planning readily, but the applicant has to be a 501(c)(3). Until one exists, a fiscal sponsor is the interim route.
And an honest limit. Grant timelines can outrun a project. If nothing is executable within a reasonable window we would fund the market study ourselves and proceed, because a study that arrives two years late has answered a question nobody is asking any more.
