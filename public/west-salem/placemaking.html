Placemaking

Home keeps going
past your front door.

What it will feel like to live here.

Now City West Salem · Willamette Riverfront · Oregon
How It Feels

Ask anyone what they want from a neighborhood, and they describe a feeling

Safe. Known. Unhurried. Yourself. This place is built around six of them.

You let your kids out the door, and your stomach stays quiet.

The courtyard is car-free and grassy, with one gate and forty kitchen windows above it. The playground is thirty seconds from your table.

Someone waves before you've ordered.

The market hall opens early and closes late. Same stalls, same stools, same faces. By March, your coffee is started when you walk in.

Time feels wider.

Coffee, school, work, dinner, and the river, all inside a ten-minute walk. The hour a day you used to spend in the car is yours again.

You come as you are, and you're equally welcome.

Wood, daylight, good chairs, warm light. Dressed up or in sandals, at eight years old or eighty, you match the room.

There's a low hum of "I could join that."

Run club Wednesdays. Open studios on the weekend. A league for every level and a stage for anyone brave enough. And a window seat above it all, for the nights you'd rather watch.

You take visitors the long way.

The river path to the tower and back adds twenty minutes to the walk home. Everyone takes it.

"Down the stairs, across the courtyard, through the market, all the way to the river. Home, the whole way."
What It's Like

Same place, different worlds

Four days from the neighborhood, a few years in. The people are invented. The place is exactly as planned.

The Family

Maya, 34, third floor on the first courtyard block

The kids go down to the courtyard on their own after breakfast. She can see them from the kitchen window, and so can four other parents, which is the point. Daycare is at the bottom of the stairs. She works two days a week at the campus, eleven minutes on foot, and buys dinner at the market hall on the way home from a farmer whose name she knows.

The thing she notices most, a year in: her attention came back. The family logistics she used to carry in her head all day got shorter, and the room that freed up filled with people.

The Elder

Walt, 71, traded a big house in Keizer for a flat by the market

He walks the river path early, when the herons are out. Lunch at the market, same stool. Two afternoons a week he runs the wood shop at the maker studios, teaching kids to sharpen a chisel, and they call him Walt, and they need him.

He knows the baristas, the butcher, the woman who runs the gallery. His old house had a big yard with only him in it. Here his life got smaller in square feet and bigger in every other way.

The Maker

Dani, 26, rents a loft near the campus

Her rent works because her car is gone. Pickleball league Mondays, run club Wednesdays along the water, and on Fridays her band plays the small stage at the food hall to forty people she mostly knows.

Everything she used to drive to Portland for happens within four blocks, and her Portland friends now come to her.

The Test

A wet Tuesday in January

Rain on the river, dark at 4:30. This is the day a neighborhood proves itself.

The market hall is full and loud and warm. Someone's kid is playing their first show. The sauna by the water has a waitlist, the winter lights are on in the courtyards, and the dark months have somewhere to be.

A Year of Saying Yes

There's always something on

Food and drink, craft, music, and sport, in every season. Come to everything or come to nothing; the seat is there either way.

Food & Drink

The table is long

  • A daily civic market and food hall, the neighborhood's living room
  • Long-table harvest dinners and farm-to-fork suppers
  • Breweries, cider, coffee, and a riverside night market
  • Seasonal tastings from the Willamette Valley's farms
Creativity & Craft

Made here, by hand

  • Artist studios, maker spaces, and open-studio weekends
  • Galleries and evening arts programming on the riverfront
  • Workshops: ceramics, wood, textile, print, and repair
  • An annual craft and design festival that draws the region
Live & Entertainment

Music on the water

  • A riverfront concert series and outdoor film nights
  • First Fridays, storytelling, and intimate live sessions
  • Seasonal festivals anchored to the Salem Forest Tower
  • Winter lights and a solstice gathering on the green
Active & Outdoors

Move every day

  • Pickleball and padel leagues, plus a sports & wellness center
  • Run and ride clubs on the river path; sunrise yoga on the green
  • Paddle days, river clean-ups, and a community 5k
  • Youth programming and four-season outdoor living

Already Starting

The neighborhood begins years before the buildings do

The market opens first. The festivals start before the cranes. By the time the first homes open, moving in feels like joining something already going. More in the Activation Plan.

Now

The first wave hello

The market, the arts nights, the first festivals. The same faces start coming back week after week, years ahead of the first homes.

The First Block

Kids out the door

The first courtyard homes open. Gardens at the center, mornings that start downstairs, and a Saturday market you can hear from bed.

The Years After

The long way around

Festivals people plan their year around, concerts on the water, the tower on the skyline. Visitors take the long way. So does everyone who lives here.
