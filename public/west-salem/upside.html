Upside Explorer: why, what & how it works

Why it exists. Built the ordinary way, many worthwhile projects lose money on paper, so they never get built. This tool shows, transparently, how a skilled team closes that gap with real, defensible moves rather than wishful assumptions.

What it is. A live financial model of a real ground-up multifamily project (it is built for rental schemes of roughly 100 homes and up). It starts from the honest base case (what the project returns built conventionally, with nothing added) and lets you stack on each real move, a more efficient building, public partnerships, affordable-housing finance, and watch the return respond instantly.

How to use it.

  1. Pick a scenario from the dark bar at the top. The numbers and charts on the right update at once.
  2. Hover any scenario button to read exactly what that scenario does.
  3. Open the input panels below to change any assumption yourself.
  4. Export any version as a branded Excel model to share or keep.

The big chart on the right is the heart of it: the return climbing from the red starting point, one kind of move at a time, so you can see what each one is worth. A planning tool for discussion, not an offer of securities; figures are estimates to be confirmed in diligence.

Glossary · terms & acronyms▶
Point Access Block (PAB)
A multifamily building where apartments cluster around a single central core (one stair, one elevator) instead of lining a double-loaded corridor with two exit stairs. Enabled by recent single-stair code reform. It reaches ~90-95% floor efficiency (vs ~80-87% for a corridor) and gives most units windows on more than one side. Tradeoffs: more cores and elevators, smaller floor plates, and it is a newer form some lenders and reviewers are still learning.
Single-stair
A building served by one exit stairway per floor, allowed under recent code reform within size limits. It is what makes the Point Access Block form possible.
Double-loaded corridor
The conventional layout: a central hallway with apartments on both sides and two exit stairs. Loses 15-20% of the floor to circulation.
Slab-on-grade
A building that sits on a concrete slab poured on the ground, avoiding an expensive structural podium or underground parking.
Dual-aspect unit
An apartment with windows on two or more sides, giving cross-ventilation and daylight; a quality and rent advantage.
Base case (honest floor)
The project's return built conventionally with no levers applied. The negative starting point everything builds from.
Lever
One real, separable move a team can make (a cost saving, a public tool, a financing structure) that changes the return.
Waterfall
The bar chart that shows the return climbing from the floor, attributing each step to a category of moves.
Levered IRR
Internal Rate of Return: the annualized return on invested equity, accounting for the timing of cash in and out. "Levered" means after debt.
Equity multiple
Total cash returned divided by cash invested. 2.0x means you get back twice what you put in.
DSCR
Debt Service Coverage Ratio: net operating income divided by the annual loan payment. Lenders want it comfortably above 1.0x.
NOI
Net Operating Income: rental and other income after operating expenses, before debt and taxes. The number a buyer capitalizes.
EGI
Effective Gross Income: all income after vacancy and credit loss, before operating expenses.
Yield on cost
Stabilized NOI divided by total project cost; the unlevered return on the dollars spent.
Exit cap rate
The rate used to convert NOI into value at sale. A lower cap rate means a higher value.
FAR
Floor Area Ratio: total building floor area divided by land area; the zoning measure of how much you can build on a site.
NRA
Net Rentable Area: the leasable square footage that earns rent, excluding hallways, lobbies, and mechanical space.
Sources & uses
The capital stack (debt, equity, subsidy) set against where it is spent (land, construction, soft costs).
Hard / soft cost
Hard cost is physical construction (labor and materials). Soft cost is design, fees, financing, and other non-construction costs.
LIHTC
Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: federal credits sold to investors to fund affordable rental housing; the backbone of US affordable-housing finance.
HUD 221(d)(4)
A federally insured construction-to-permanent loan with a 40-year term, high leverage, and non-recourse terms.
TIF
Tax Increment Financing: a public tool that reimburses eligible project costs from the future property-tax growth the project creates.
NMTC
New Markets Tax Credit: a federal credit that subsidizes commercial development in qualifying areas.
C-PACE
Long-term financing for energy and resiliency scope, repaid through a property assessment that stays with the building at sale.
MUPTE / Vertical Housing abatement
Oregon programs that reduce property tax for a set period to encourage dense, mixed-income housing.
Opportunity Zone
A federal program that defers and reduces capital-gains tax for long-term investment in designated areas.
Ground lease
Leasing the land rather than buying it, lowering upfront cost in exchange for ongoing rent.
Placemaking premium
The modest rent uplift a well-designed, amenity-rich place can earn as it matures.
Lease-up / stabilization
The period after opening when a building fills to its long-run occupancy.
Affordable sleeve
The share of homes set aside at restricted, below-market rents, often funded with LIHTC.
