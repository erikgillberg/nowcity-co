Now City & Denmark · A Working Plan · August 2026
The Goal

Start with what
liveable means

Most liveable city in America is a big claim for a modest state capital, so it helps to say what we mean. Liveability is measured in ordinary days: a home you can afford near the things you love, trips that work without a car, streets that hold public life, buildings that keep you healthy, nature doing real work, culture and play within walking distance. These are not amenities in the shallow sense. They are demand drivers, health infrastructure, and the foundation of long-term asset value.

Copenhagen has sat at or near the top of the global liveability rankings for years, and the reason is method: fifty years of it, carried by a national community of designers, engineers, builders, investors, and public institutions. Salem has the raw material: a walkable core, a riverfront, universities, state government, land that can still be shaped. What it has lacked is the method.

The deeper question is bigger than Salem. Communities everywhere need neighborhoods that are regenerative, resilient, and equitable: mixed use, mixed income, multigenerational, built to take on climate change through regenerative design and to handle shocks through circular systems and green infrastructure, with ownership models that let more of the people who live in a place own a piece of it. The problem is that the current development model makes such places unusually hard to finance, approve, and build. So the honest version of our question is this: what would it take to make them buildable, repeatably, starting somewhere real? Salem is where the first proof can stand.

The Product

What would Denmark design?

A neighborhood as a product of Denmark, the way a great piece of Danish design carries its origin as a mark of quality. Take the thought experiment seriously: ask Denmark's best designers and engineers to deliver a neighborhood the way Denmark delivers a product.

They would design it as one system. Buildings shaped for daylight. Envelopes that make Passive House performance ordinary. Heating and cooling moved by machinery Denmark already manufactures. Water treated as a resource: captured, cleaned, reused, and enjoyed. Streets where walking is the easy choice and transit makes land more valuable. Public space doing the social work it does in Copenhagen every day. Every material carried in a whole-life carbon budget, and the whole thing designed to be maintained, adapted, and eventually taken apart rather than demolished.

None of that is speculative in Denmark. It is building code, product catalog, and daily practice. The new work is engineering that practice to American codes, costs, and construction culture: a product development problem. That framing matters, because product development is something Denmark and America both know how to organize, fund, and run.

What Denmark's method has been missing in America is the unit of proof that changes minds at scale: a whole district, designed as one system.

The Teacher

What Copenhagen has learned

A product of Denmark should carry what Denmark has actually learned. Three generations of it are relevant here:

The past

Plan first, grow along the plan

The 1947 Finger Plan pointed Copenhagen's growth along transit lines with nature between them, and the city has compounded on that decision for seventy-five years. The harbor went from industrial sewer to swimmable in a generation because the city treated water as public wealth. Lesson: early structural decisions do most of the work.

The present

Code, catalog, and craft

Nordhavn runs as a five-minute city on district energy. Denmark wrote whole-life carbon limits into its national building code before anyone else. The metro is financed by the land value it creates. Lesson: liveability is now an engineering and financing discipline, documented well enough to travel.

The future

Success brings its own pressures

Copenhagen is still working on affordability under its own popularity, retrofit at scale, and wetter storms. Lesson: bring the unsolved problems along too. A demonstration district that engages what Copenhagen is still learning is a better partner than one that only copies its victories.

The Supply Chain

What ships, and what gets made here

A product is only as real as its supply chain, so it is worth being concrete about what would actually move across the Atlantic.

Ships in containers

The high-value, compact systems Denmark already exports: heat pumps and controls, ventilation cores, window and daylight assemblies, specialized fixtures. Small enough to ship, valuable enough to matter, and branded enough to carry the product's origin.

Made in America

The heavy and bulky layers want local production: envelopes and insulation, structural elements, prefabricated assemblies. Danish industry already operates American factories, and a near-site micro-factory is part of the product itself: local jobs building Danish-engineered components.

Ships free

The most valuable layer weighs nothing: the designs, specifications, standards, digital models, and measurement protocols that carry the intelligence. This layer is what makes the hundredth district cheaper and better than the first.

Which system lands in which layer is real engineering-economics work, and it is exactly what the design year exists to settle, with the makers in the room.
The Region

How one district becomes many

Here is the part most plans skip. A product needs something to plug into, and most of what makes district development slow in America is the one-off nature of everything around it: entitlements negotiated from scratch, utility connections handled as exceptions, financing reinvented for every project.

So the regional work is to design the connection points once. Entitlement templates a city can adopt. Utility interfaces for district energy and water designed as standards rather than negotiations. A supplier and workforce network that carries from one district to the next. Capital templates that repeat. Think of it as the socket the product snaps into.

Salem proves the socket. The Willamette Valley, with Portland an hour north and a string of mid-sized cities along the corridor, becomes the first region where districts can repeat. Other regions copy the pattern, and the second region is easier than the first because the socket, like the product, is documented.

Capital

Who pays for what

The money works on one clean separation, drawn sharply so it is easy to correct. Denmark backs the product: the development effort where Danish upside lives, supported by industry product budgets, foundation missions, export finance, and the platform institutions. America funds the project: Edgewater capitalizes as an American real estate project from American and international sources, on its own track. And the project buys the product: Phase One specifies Danish systems where they win on merit and purchases them at market. A paid installation, and a channel that compounds with every district that follows.

Now City's side of that bargain is already standing: master developer of Edgewater, twenty-two riverfront acres under agreement, a committed first-phase partnership, an entitlement path, and a four-phase plan across roughly a decade. The district proceeds on its own strength, which is what makes it a credible first customer rather than a hopeful one.

And there is a larger pool watching. The world's biggest retirement funds increasingly carry mandates to place capital in beneficial, resilient assets, and Danish funds helped invent that practice. What those mandates chronically lack is supply: investable units of the thing they are mandated to want. A district product with published performance, a repeatable playbook, and a pipeline of cities is designed to be exactly that unit. Whether it clears their bar is a question only the people who hold the mandates can answer, and several of them are close to the people on this page. We would like help asking.

Said in one breath: Denmark backs the product. America funds the project. The project buys the product. This is the part of the plan we believe most and have tested least, which is why it goes first into the room.
The Demand

A hundred cities like Salem

America is short millions of homes, and the shortage concentrates in exactly the kind of city Salem is: mid-sized, growing, priced out of its own future, and short of the delivery capacity to build its way back.

That is the demand side of this partnership, and it is Now City's job. The playbook, the site pipeline, the underwriting tools, and the story that makes the next city want a district product rather than another subdivision. Salem first. Then the corridor. Then the hundred American cities facing the same decade. If demand reaches even a fraction of that, the product effort stops being a project and becomes an export industry, and the coalition that designed it holds the deepest expertise in it.

The Builders

Who builds it, and how it stays coherent

The product effort needs a home on each shore: the Edgewater innovation district in Salem, working in the Newlab lineage as the adaptation lab, landing pad, and measurement engine; and BLOXHUB in Copenhagen, the natural convening room, with two of this coalition's people in its leadership. One product lab, two rooms, one agenda.

And a fair worry, heard already: this many contributors sounds like chaos. The usual answer is one owner, one design team, one vision. It is coherent, and it produces the same place every time. The districts we admire most were built a different way, on four working rules:

A shared code

The masterplan and the district code set the streets, the heights, the materials, and the carbon standard. Inside the code, every building gets its own author. Nordhavn, Bo01, and the IBA districts were drawn by dozens of studios, one building each, inside one code. That is why they feel coherent and alive at the same time.

Clear decision rights

Wide conversations, narrow decisions. Contributors own their piece fully, and Now City holds land, entitlement, standards, procurement, capital design, and story, the way IBA's office of thirty curated 120 projects built by others.

Open, then closed

Divergence gets a calendar: the design year, the studios, the competitions. Convergence gets a deadline: the Phase One specification. Everyone always knows which mode the room is in.

One phase at a time

The district builds over a decade, and each phase involves a handful of firms working inside the code. Coordination stays human-sized the whole way.

The Invitation

An Expression of Interest

Everything above is a working hypothesis, and hypotheses earn the right to become real through the people who sign on to test them. So here is what we are asking for now, and it is deliberately small.

If this page describes work you want to see exist, sign an Expression of Interest. It is one page. It is non-binding. It says: this idea deserves serious formation, and I want a hand in shaping the part I know best, whether that is design, engineering, supply, capital, convening, civic partnership, or something we have missed. Signatures tell us, and each other, that the room is worth assembling, and they shape the agenda of the first convening and the design year that follows.

If someone sent you this page, the invitation includes you. The coalition drawn here is a beginning, and the map has room.

Act I

Listen and sign

Now through spring 2027. Expressions of Interest gather, and the plan goes through the people signing it: working sessions where the coalition takes it apart and redraws it. Alongside: the Trade Council conversation, a first Danish working visit to Salem, a first convening at BLOXHUB.

Act II

The design year

2027. The product takes shape against a real site: masterplan review, the whole-life carbon standard, system selection, supply-chain decisions, and the mobility strategy for the district's center. The year closes with the work exhibited in both cities and Phase One carrying the product's first order.

Act III

The demonstration

2028 through 2031. Phase One is built and the product delivered: Danish systems installed together, measured together, published together. Study tours run while the cranes are still up.

Act IV

The industry

2031 onward. The playbook, supplier network, and standards travel to the next district and the next region. The partnership matures into something Denmark's official trade-promotion platform could recognize as its own, at its initiative.
