Designed with Denmark. Built for America. A partnership to prove that Danish urbanism, delivered as a product, can make an American capital city the most liveable in its country. Starting in Salem, Oregon.

Now City & Denmark · The Working Plan · August 2026
The Big Idea

A neighborhood that is a product of Denmark

Now City is building a North American platform for neighborhood-scale regenerative development, and West Salem is its demonstration district: Danish architecture, engineering, energy, water, mobility, construction, technology, and urban design working together inside one commercially replicable American neighborhood. The framing is literal: a Now City Neighborhood as a product of Denmark, the way a great piece of Danish design carries its origin as a mark of quality.

Denmark has spent two decades building institutions whose purpose is connecting Danish urban expertise to international projects: the Trade Council and its Sustainable Urban Development advisory, State of Green, the Danish Architecture Center, the Danish Design Center, DenmarkBridge. A consortium of Danish firms, universities, investors, and public institutions assembled around one American district is precisely the shape these platforms exist to support, and the concept aligns with the public priorities of His Majesty King Frederik X, whose patronages span these exact institutions. That alignment is held with care: recognition is earned through the institutions, never sought as endorsement.

The Ambition

Liveability is a method, and the method is Danish

The ambition: make Salem, Oregon the most liveable city in Oregon, and in time a serious contender for the most liveable city in the United States.

Salem is Oregon's capital, a city of nearly two hundred thousand people on the Willamette River, an hour south of Portland. It has the raw material liveability is made from: a walkable core, a riverfront, universities, state government, and land that can still be shaped. What it has lacked is a method.

Liveability is measured in ordinary days: a home you can afford near the things you love, trips that do not require a car, streets that hold public life, buildings that keep you healthy, nature that works as infrastructure, culture and play within walking distance. The cities that top the global liveability indexes score on exactly these things, and Copenhagen sits at or near the top year after year. That is not chance. It is a method, refined over fifty years, carried by a national ecosystem of designers, engineers, builders, investors, and public institutions. That method is exportable. Denmark exports it deliberately. What it has never had in America is the unit of proof that changes minds at scale: a whole district, designed as one system, on American soil, under American rules, at American costs.

The Model

A product, and its first customer

The partnership stands on one clean separation, and it is the reason this works for both sides.

1

Denmark backs the product

The Danish District is a product: an integrated, exportable package of architecture, energy, water, envelopes, daylight, mobility, public realm, standards, and supply chain, engineered to American codes and costs. The coalition develops it. Danish support flows to product development, where Danish upside lives: industry product budgets, foundation missions, export-finance instruments, and the national platform institutions doing what they were built to do.

2

America funds the project

Edgewater capitalizes as an American real estate project, on its own track, from American and international sources: platform capital, the district fund, and phase capitalizations. The district proceeds on its own strength. Danish partners are invited into a project that is already moving, never asked to carry it.

3

The project buys the product

Procurement is the bridge. Phase One specifies Danish systems where they win on merit and purchases them at market: the demonstration is a paid installation, not a subsidized one. Every district that follows re-buys the product, and the product improves with every purchase. That is a channel, not a charity.

Said in one breath: Denmark backs the product. America funds the project. The project buys the product.

The Engine

The innovation district builds the product

Every product needs a place where it gets developed, tested, and shown. This plan has two, one on each side of the Atlantic, and standing them up is the strategy's first move.

In Salem: the Edgewater innovation district. Edgewater's plan already carries an innovation district in the studio-lab lineage of Newlab: applied research and enterprise working inside a real neighborhood. In this partnership it becomes the American home of the Danish District product effort: the adaptation lab where Danish systems meet US codes, costs, and construction culture; the landing pad where Danish companies and SMEs get an American address, a demonstration block, and their first reference customers; and the measurement engine that instruments the district and publishes what the systems actually do. The product is developed in the same place that buys it.

In Copenhagen: BLOXHUB. The mirror room: 600+ members working on sustainable urbanization, two coalition members in its leadership, and the natural host for the product effort's Danish side: convenings, studio sessions, and member calls against district challenges.

One product lab, two rooms, one agenda. Chartering it is Act One.

The Team

A team at the top

Plans like this are not executed by committees. They are executed by a small team at the top with the authority to develop the plan, run the coalition, and sell the product on both sides of the Atlantic.

On the American side that team is Now City, holding the site, the city, the capital, and the delivery. On the Danish side, the plan calls for a leadership team of two. One who orchestrates: a leader who has delivered complex civic infrastructure inside Danish institutions, knows from the inside how public innovation happens, and can run a room that spans ministries, federations, studios, and companies. One who leads urban strategy: a leader fluent in the discipline that binds transit, land value, and daily life into one plan, because that discipline is the spine of the product itself. Together they are the partnership's executive core: the ones who take this document, sharpen it, and own it.

Everything below is written for that team.

Now City's job is to make the big crazy idea orchestrable: to hold the American side so firmly that the Danish side only has to hold the product.

That position is what makes the district a credible first customer, and it is not contingent on this partnership.

The levers

Orchestration is real because the levers are real. Now City holds six: land (what the district stands on), entitlement (what is allowed to be built), standards (what performance is required), procurement (what gets bought: the lever that purchases the product), capital design (how each phase is funded and what it must prove), and story (what the district means, told well enough that cities and capital want the next one). The Danish coalition plugs into all six without having to own any of them.

How it scales. Salem proves the product at full district scale. The playbook, the supplier consortium, the measured results, and the coalition itself then travel to the next Now City district, where the product is bought again at version two. Denmark's export channel compounds with each district; Now City's platform compounds with each product improvement. Neither side scales without the other, which is what makes the partnership durable.

The Arc

Four acts

The arc runs in four acts. Dates are targets, not promises; the sequence is the commitment.

Act I · Formation (now through spring 2027). The leadership team forms and takes ownership of this plan. The product lab is chartered in its two rooms: the Edgewater innovation district and BLOXHUB. The Trade Council's Sustainable Urban Development program is engaged as institutional anchor. A first Danish working visit walks the Salem site; a first Copenhagen convening assembles the coalition. The goal of Act I is not size; it is the right dozen commitments and a credible anchor engaged.

Act II · The Design Year (2027). The product takes shape against a real site: masterplan review, the city-nature and public-realm strategy, the whole-life carbon standard, system and product selection, and the mobility and urban strategy for the district's center. A Danish delegation visits Salem; Salem's leadership visits Copenhagen. The year closes with the plan exhibited publicly in both cities, and with the Phase One specification carrying the product's first order.

Act III · The Demonstration (2028 through 2031). Phase One is built and the product is delivered: Danish systems installed together, measured together, published together. Study tours run while the cranes are still up. The district becomes the American reference case Danish industry has never had.

Act IV · Replication (2031 onward). The playbook, the supplier consortium, and the standards travel to the next Now City district, and the partnership matures into something Denmark's official trade-promotion platform can recognize as its own. This is the stage at which a royal business delegation or project visit becomes appropriate to explore, through the patron institutions, at their initiative.
