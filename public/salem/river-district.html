The Vision

A district on both banks of the Willamette

What Salem already has, what is missing, and what could be built on the river to close the gap.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
Before You Start

What this page is

This page is a proposal, drawn at district scale so it is specific enough to discuss. It shows what the ground could hold and what daily life in it could be like. The home page explains why we published it, and the study plan sets out the studies that would test whether any of it holds up.

The Starting Position

What Salem already has

These conditions are in place today and are mostly under-used.

A river through the middle

The Willamette runs between downtown and West Salem, with public land, an island park and two bridges. Most of the frontage is currently industrial or fenced.

Capital city institutions

State government, a regional hospital system, universities within reach, and community colleges running programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics. Employment here is steadier through downturns than in comparable cities.

A position on the corridor

Salem is within an hour of Portland, the Hillsboro semiconductor cluster, Oregon State and Eugene. It is the only point on that line within reach of all four, and the seat of state government.

An existing industrial base

Food processing, wood products, logistics, advanced manufacturing and a humanoid robotics factory. The jobs span several wage levels.

Land and relative affordability

Developable land inside the urban growth boundary, industrial buildings suitable for adaptation, and prices that still allow ownership on local wages.

Supportive adopted policy

Salem's comprehensive plan calls for housing near major employment centers, mixed-use riverfront development and support for emerging industries. The policy basis exists.

Conditions Today

What is missing today

These are five conditions a district on the river would have to change. Each is addressable, and each takes longer than one project to fix.

01

Walkable density outside a few downtown blocks

Most of the city is built at a scale that requires a car for every trip, which limits street life and raises household transport costs.

02

A working riverfront

There is no continuous public edge along the water, and few places to eat, work, live or launch a boat.

03

Housing delivery at the pace the state now requires

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions. The open question is delivery capacity.

04

Reasons for young people to stay

Salem educates people who then move to Portland or further for work, for rents they can justify and for a wider social life.

05

A destination on each side of the river

West Salem is largely residential and downtown empties in the evening, so the bridges carry commuters in the morning and again at five.

The Proposition

A district built around the river

Salem has a river through the middle of it and very little built to face the water. The area under study runs along both sides of it, because a continuous public edge, a crossing built for people and shared infrastructure only make sense at that scale. An old railroad bridge already links the two sides and carries people on foot and on bikes.

At the bridges

The downtown waterfront on one side and the industrial blocks on the other, treated as one place rather than two. Workspace, homes, a market and a public edge along the water.

Downstream

A large area of riverfront land, most of it undeveloped, which gives the work room to continue once the first phases are complete.

Work, On The Ground

The work the district could hold

The employment here could span several wage levels. Fabrication, food production, maker space and light industry, research and engineering. That range matters, because a district has to house the people who work in it.

Large-format industrial development belongs at Mill Creek Corporate Center on the east side of the city, which is already built and marketed for it. This district holds the smaller-format work that fits beside housing: workshops, assembly, studios and labs in buildings that already stand.

Make building components

Oregon has a mass-timber sector, a federal Tech Hub for it, and an established wood products industry in the valley. The regional housing shortage is the demand.

Retain graduates

Community college and university programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics already operate here. What is required is local work and housing within reach of it.

House the workforce

Employment growth on the east side of the city creates housing demand that the current supply does not meet. This district is within reach of it.

Living Here

How the homes and blocks could work

The homes here are drawn from Scandinavian courtyard urbanism and Japanese garden philosophy: family-friendly density wrapped around quiet interior gardens, with shops and daily life at the street edge. Light-filled rooms, natural materials and high-performance building systems, in blocks people can imagine renting in, owning in, and staying in.

A Quiet Center

Interior gardens with kid-safe green space - calm at the heart of density, supervision built into everyday life.

An Active Edge

Experiential retail, cafés, and innovation workspace face the street - daily errands on foot, neighbors by default.

A Home That Works

Studios to three-bedroom townhomes; high-performance systems that lower energy bills and support everyday wellness.

Inside the Home

How the homes are built

Light-filled homes blend natural materials and Nordic simplicity with high-performance building systems that improve comfort, reduce energy costs, and support everyday wellness.

The Ground Floor

What the ground floors hold

The ground floors are programmed around the parts of life that happen every day - childcare, wellness, food, and small business - so caregiving, connection, and community are built into the block rather than driven to. Long tables, shared gardens, and front doors on the street make neighbors the default.

Landmarks & Mobility

Two ideas worth testing

A district works when people have a reason to come and can move without friction. This district proposes one landmark that pulls the region in - and one mobility system that knits the city together.

The Landmark

The Salem Forest Tower

A spiraling timber tower visible from I-5, with a continuous ramp that tells the story of the place as you climb: Native American history, the valley's biodiversity, the living systems of the river. You emerge at the top to see the Willamette running north and south with new eyes. A daily ritual for locals; a destination for everyone else.

  • Precedent: EFFEKT's Camp Adventure Forest Tower in Denmark - a 45 m observation tower and 900 m treetop boardwalk, fully accessible by continuous ramp, engineered with ARUP and completed in 2017
  • The Danish developers report ~300,000 annual visits and that the tower paid for itself in its first year of operation
  • Salem's version: regional draw from 12,100 to 41,200 vehicles a day on Wallace Road and the I-5 corridor - anchoring tourism, retail, and hospitality demand across the district
The Mobility System

Whoosh: a transit circulator born at Google

Swyft Cities' Whoosh is a cable-based network of five-passenger, battery-electric autonomous vehicles - 30 mph, with offline stations so every trip is nonstop and a vehicle is always waiting. It soars over the obstacles that divide Salem: Highway 22, the Willamette, Pringle Creek, the rail line. A concept sketch for Salem maps 16 stations across 4.5 route miles linking West Salem, downtown, Riverfront Park, and Willamette University.

  • At Irvine's 1,300-acre Great Park, the City's independent travel demand forecast projects 27,000 daily riders - with 4x the ridership of a traditional circulator at one-sixth the operating cost per trip
  • Irvine City Council selected Whoosh as the Great Park's primary transportation technology in April 2025
  • For Salem: no-wait, faster-than-car trips; less parking, which frees land for other uses; an iconic rider experience that is itself an attraction

Source: "The Potential for a Whoosh Transit Circulator in Salem, OR" (Now City & partners, 2026) and the City of Irvine Great Park travel demand analysis cited therein.

Both concepts are in early exploration with partners and public agencies. Both are early concepts explored with partners and public agencies, and the district does not depend on either.
For Salem & Oregon

Built in service of the people who are already here

The district is being designed for Salem: the families, workers, and businesses who live in the Willamette Valley today, and the generations who will inherit it. Every phase is measured against a simple question: does this serve the community and the region, now and for the long term?

Why Salem - nature, access, and an undervalued quality of life

Salem sits in the middle of the Willamette Valley, one of the most productive farming regions in the country. The coast and the Cascades are both about an hour away, housing costs less than in Portland, and the state capital and its institutions are here.

45 minutes to Portland - international airport, talent, culture - and 60 minutes to the Oregon Coast.

Strong in-migration from the expensive West Coast metros. Oregon ranked #1 in the nation for inbound movers in 2025, and Salem ranked #7 among U.S. metros, while Portland ranked 30th (United Van Lines).

Undervalued relative to quality of life and long-term upside - the gap this district is built to honor, not exploit.

City of Salem Alignment

In partnership with the city

Creating value from underused industrial land through coordinated, mixed-use district redevelopment.

  • Expands Salem’s long-term tax base while preserving near-term employment and enabling gradual land use transition
  • Delivers new housing supply calibrated to local incomes, workforce needs, and demographic demand
  • Activates the Willamette Riverfront and strengthens west–east connectivity through targeted infrastructure sequencing
  • Reduces long-term infrastructure strain by prioritizing walkability, proximity, and daily services
  • Phases density, traffic, and investment in alignment with absorption and performance thresholds
  • Positions Salem to capture regional growth while maintaining local control and fiscal discipline
Oregon State Alignment

In service of the state’s future

Producing economic growth at district scale that individual projects do not reach.

  • A coordinated urban development initiative that strengthens housing supply, job creation, and long-term tax base growth at once
  • A phased, infrastructure-aligned approach supporting housing delivery, workforce stability, and private investment confidence
  • Designed to align with statewide housing production goals, economic development priorities, and talent retention strategies
  • Advances efficient use of Opportunity Zone and urban renewal tools without expanding public balance-sheet risk
  • Supports construction, small business formation, and innovation-sector employment across the Willamette Valley
  • A replicable district framework for mid-sized Oregon cities seeking durable, market-driven growth
These commitments carry through the whole plan - the impact & placemaking framework below, the activation strategy that opens the site to the community years before vertical construction, and the phasing discipline that ties growth to real absorption.
What The District Would Try To Do

Six things we would design for

Now City builds neighbourhoods that are meant to leave the land, the water and the people around them better off than they were. These are the six things that would mean in practice here, and each one is something a study or a measurement could check.

Clean air, water and soil

Stormwater cleaned on the block before it reaches the river. The bank replanted. Tree canopy and habitat added rather than removed. These are measurable, and they are the local environment people actually live in.

Homes that hold up under stress

Buildings, energy, water and food supply designed to keep working through a heat wave, a flood or a power outage, so households are safer and the wider city has somewhere that still functions.

A place that works for everyone in a household

Designed around caregiving, small children, older residents and people who do not drive. A place that works for a seven-year-old and an eighty-year-old usually works for everyone in between.

Public space and daily life

The parts of a neighbourhood people use every day, put close enough together that a day takes fewer car trips.

  • Streets built for walking with shade, planting and places to sit, so short trips get made on foot
  • Food and drink at ground level, breweries, a food hall, small shops and year-round programming
  • Workshops and studios in the same blocks as homes, so making things stays visible in daily life
  • Safe bike routes and a transit connection to downtown Salem, the riverfront and the wider region

A district that keeps working in an emergency

A place where people already gather, with its own power, heat and water systems, is useful to a city on the worst day of the year. That capacity comes from ordinary civic buildings doing a second job rather than from anything labelled for emergencies.

  1. Sports halls and event spaces that can serve as gathering points when the city needs them
  2. District-scale power, heating, cooling and water systems that keep running when the wider network is under strain
  3. Homes within walking distance of daily needs, so a disruption to driving does not cut people off from food and care

Food, from the valley to the block

The Willamette Valley is one of the most diverse farming regions in the country, and Salem sits in the middle of it. A district here could connect that farming to processing, markets and daily meals in one place.

  1. Regional farms connected to on-site processing and markets, shortening the distance between a field and a kitchen
  2. Space for food and soil businesses to test things, for growers, researchers and small producers
  3. Food waste composted and returned to regional farms rather than sent to landfill
  4. A model other towns in the valley could copy if it works here

Where new technology would be put to work

Oregon already has companies and researchers building tools that act on the physical world. A district gives them somewhere real to apply that work, on problems people can see. These are the five we would start with.

  1. Making buildings use less energy and fewer materials
  2. Making it easier to get around without a car
  3. Measuring water use, waste and energy so the district can see how it is doing
  4. Supporting food production and tracking the district's carbon
  5. Improving public health services, safety and everyday care

Landscape doing real work

Planting here would be built to perform, in the same way a street or a pipe performs. It cools the air, holds and cleans water, and supports habitat, at the same time as being somewhere pleasant to sit.

  1. Planting integrated into streets, plazas and open space as part of how the district works
  2. Cooling and stormwater handled by the landscape, which reduces what the city has to build and maintain
  3. Habitat and public space on the same ground, along the bank and through the blocks
  4. Open space that can change with the seasons and as the district grows

Designed around women, children and older residents

Places designed around the daily realities of women, children and older adults tend to do better on health, safety and how long people stay. Designing for the people with the least margin produces a place that works for everyone.

  1. Homes and services that let people age in place, which keeps independence longer and costs the health system less
  2. A public realm children can use, with safe routes, places to play and enough activity on the street that they are watched
  3. Safety, access and care designed in from the start, which also tends to improve walking and local trade
  4. Households of different ages in the same blocks, which reduces isolation and keeps people living here longer
