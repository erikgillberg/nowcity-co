A Proposal for Study

How might Salem become the most livable city in America by 2050?

A district on the Willamette in Salem, and the studies that would test whether it works.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
Our Interest

Why we are writing this down

We are a development company. We want to build in Salem, and we would like other private capital to invest here alongside us.

Private capital does not arrive in a place until some basic questions have been answered, and in Salem several of them have not been. What the regional economy could support. What the distance between the valley's cities costs the people and employers along it. What kind of housing local incomes actually sustain, and who could build it at the pace the state now requires.

Without those answers every project here is underwritten as though it were the first one, which is slow and expensive, and it is part of why good sites sit for years. So instead of waiting for someone else to produce them, we are proposing the questions be studied openly, by people with no stake in the outcome, and published in full. If the answers are encouraging we build, and so can others. If they are discouraging, everybody finds out cheaply.

The Test

How might this region grow in a way that improves lives and ecosystems?

Salem has about 183,000 residents and has been adding people steadily for decades. It will keep growing whether or not anyone plans for it. Growth by itself does nothing for livability, and in most American cities it works against it: more cars, longer trips, more farmland consumed, more runoff into the river. That outcome follows from where the homes go rather than from how many people arrive.

Traffic

Households in walkable, mixed-use areas make fewer and shorter vehicle trips than households at the edge of a city. Homes placed near work, schools, shops and transit reduce total driving even as the population rises.

Daily life

Proximity is what gives people time back. A shorter commute, a school within walking distance and a market on the way home change how a day is spent more than any single amenity does.

Local ecology

Reusing buildings that already stand consumes no new land, and compact development leaves farmland and habitat intact. At district scale, stormwater can be cleaned before it reaches the river, the bank can be replanted, and canopy and habitat added rather than removed.

Say Salem added thirty thousand residents over the next twenty years. That is close to its existing trend line, so the number is not the difficult part. Whether those homes go at the edge or in the middle decides whether the city that results is better to live in than the one we have now. That is what a district like this is meant to test, and the studies at the end of this page are how it would be measured.

What Needs Answering

Three questions underneath it

Each of these is a public question rather than a private one. Each has a natural sponsor better placed to hold it than we are. And each is unanswered today in a way that costs the region money.

Work

How might Salem and Oregon support small business and build on the physical AI work already here?

The valley already holds the pieces: a semiconductor cluster to the north, a robotics lineage at Oregon State, one of the most diverse farm valleys in the country, a health system, a mass-timber sector, and a humanoid robot factory in Salem. What it lacks is a place where they meet and a path for the small companies that grow up around them.

Natural sponsors: Business Oregon, the regional economic development organization, the universities and colleges, employers. The long version.

Movement

How might the friction along the Portland, Salem, Corvallis and Eugene corridor be reduced?

Four cities inside about 110 miles that behave as four separate labor markets. Someone living in Corvallis and a job in Hillsboro are two hours apart in practice. That gap is a real cost to employers and households, and nobody has priced it.

Natural sponsors: ODOT, the councils of government, the corridor cities. The rail corridor and district mobility.

Homes

How might Salem and the region meet the housing shortage?

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions, so the strategy exists. Delivery capacity is the open question, and so is which housing types local incomes actually support.

Natural sponsors: Oregon Housing and Community Services, DLCD, the City, philanthropy. The long version.

Most regions study jobs, movement and housing separately and never watch them interact, which is part of why the answers so rarely compound. A district is the one instrument where all three can be observed together, on the same ground, at the same time. That is the argument for building one.
The Starting Position

What Salem already has

These conditions are in place today and are mostly under-used.

A river through the middle

The Willamette runs between downtown and West Salem, with public land, an island park and two bridges. Most of the frontage is currently industrial or fenced.

Capital city institutions

State government, a regional hospital system, universities within reach, and community colleges running programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics. Employment here is steadier through downturns than in comparable cities.

A position on the corridor

Salem is within an hour of Portland, the Hillsboro semiconductor cluster, Oregon State and Eugene. It is the only point on that line within reach of all four, and the seat of state government.

An existing industrial base

Food processing, wood products, logistics, advanced manufacturing and a humanoid robotics factory. The jobs span several wage levels.

Land and relative affordability

Developable land inside the urban growth boundary, industrial buildings suitable for adaptation, and prices that still allow ownership on local wages.

Supportive adopted policy

Salem's comprehensive plan calls for housing near major employment centers, mixed-use riverfront development and support for emerging industries. The policy basis exists.

The Gaps

What is missing today

Five conditions stand between Salem and the question on the cover. Each is addressable, and each takes longer than one project.

01
Walkable density outside a few downtown blocks

Most of the city is built at a scale that requires a car for every trip, which limits street life and raises household transport costs.

02
A working riverfront

There is no continuous public edge along the water, and few places to eat, work, live or launch a boat.

03
Housing delivery at the pace the state now requires

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions. The open question is delivery capacity.

04
Reasons for young people to stay

Salem educates people who then move to Portland or further for work, for rents they can justify and for a wider social life.

05
A destination on each side of the river

West Salem is largely residential and downtown empties in the evening, so the bridges carry commuters in the morning and again at five.

The Proposition

A district built around the river

Salem has a river through the middle of it and very little built to face the water. The area under study runs along both sides of it, because a continuous public edge, a crossing built for people and shared infrastructure only make sense at that scale. An old railroad bridge already links the two sides and carries people on foot and on bikes.

At the bridges

The downtown waterfront on one side and the industrial blocks on the other, treated as one place rather than two. Workspace, homes, a market and a public edge along the water.

Downstream

A large area of riverfront land, most of it undeveloped, which gives the work room to continue once the first phases are complete.

Work, On The Ground

The work the district could hold

The employment here could span several wage levels. Fabrication, food production, maker space and light industry, research and engineering. That range matters, because a district has to house the people who work in it.

Large-format industrial development belongs at Mill Creek Corporate Center on the east side of the city, which is already built and marketed for it. This district holds the smaller-format work that fits beside housing: workshops, assembly, studios and labs in buildings that already stand.

Make building components

Oregon has a mass-timber sector, a federal Tech Hub for it, and an established wood products industry in the valley. The regional housing shortage is the demand.

Retain graduates

Community college and university programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics already operate here. What is required is local work and housing within reach of it.

House the workforce

Employment growth on the east side of the city creates housing demand the city has not yet met. This district is within reach of it.

Landmarks & Mobility

Two ideas worth testing

A district works when people have a reason to come and can move without friction. This district proposes one landmark that pulls the region in - and one mobility system that knits the city together.

The Landmark

The Salem Forest Tower

A spiraling timber tower visible from I-5 - and a journey, not just a view. As you climb the continuous ramp, the story of this place unfolds around you: Native American history, the valley's biodiversity, the living systems of the river. You emerge at the top to see the Willamette running north and south with new eyes. A daily ritual for locals; a destination for everyone else.

  • Precedent: EFFEKT's Camp Adventure Forest Tower in Denmark - a 45 m observation tower and 900 m treetop boardwalk, fully accessible by continuous ramp, engineered with ARUP and completed in 2017
  • The Danish developers report ~300,000 annual visits and that the tower paid for itself in its first year of operation
  • Salem's version: regional draw from 12,100–41,200 vehicles/day on Wallace Road and the I-5 corridor - anchoring tourism, retail, and hospitality demand across the district
The Mobility System

Whoosh: a transit circulator born at Google

Swyft Cities' Whoosh is a cable-based network of five-passenger, battery-electric autonomous vehicles - 30 mph, with offline stations so every trip is nonstop and a vehicle is always waiting. It soars over the obstacles that divide Salem: Highway 22, the Willamette, Pringle Creek, the rail line. A concept sketch for Salem maps 16 stations across 4.5 route miles linking West Salem, downtown, Riverfront Park, and Willamette University.

  • At Irvine's 1,300-acre Great Park, the City's independent travel demand forecast projects 27,000 daily riders - with 4x the ridership of a traditional circulator at one-sixth the operating cost per trip
  • Irvine City Council selected Whoosh as the Great Park's primary transportation technology in April 2025
  • For Salem: no-wait, faster-than-car trips; parking optimization that unlocks land value; an iconic rider experience that is itself an attraction

Source: "The Potential for a Whoosh Transit Circulator in Salem, OR" (Now City & partners, 2026) and the City of Irvine Great Park travel demand analysis cited therein.

Both concepts are in early exploration with partners and public agencies. Both are early concepts explored with partners and public agencies, and the district does not depend on either.
For Salem & Oregon

Built in service of the people who are already here

This district is not an island. It is being designed for Salem - the families, workers, and businesses who call the Willamette Valley home today, and the generations who will inherit it. Every phase is measured against a simple question: does this serve the community and the region, now and for the long term?

Why Salem - nature, access, and an undervalued quality of life

Salem sits at the heart of the Willamette River Valley, one of the most livable and productive regions in the U.S. It offers rare proximity to world-class nature, a growing innovation economy, and a lower-cost, under-recognized lifestyle advantage.

45 minutes to Portland - international airport, talent, culture - and 60 minutes to the Oregon Coast.

Strong in-migration from the expensive West Coast metros. Oregon ranked #1 in the nation for inbound movers in 2025, and Salem ranked #7 among U.S. metros, while Portland ranked 30th (United Van Lines).

Undervalued relative to quality of life and long-term upside - the gap this district is built to honor, not exploit.

City of Salem Alignment

In partnership with the city

Unlocking value from underutilized industrial land through coordinated, mixed-use district redevelopment.

  • Expands Salem’s long-term tax base while preserving near-term employment and enabling gradual land use transition
  • Delivers new housing supply calibrated to local incomes, workforce needs, and demographic demand
  • Activates the Willamette Riverfront and strengthens west–east connectivity through targeted infrastructure sequencing
  • Reduces long-term infrastructure strain by prioritizing walkability, proximity, and daily services
  • Phases density, traffic, and investment in alignment with absorption and performance thresholds
  • Positions Salem to capture regional growth while maintaining local control and fiscal discipline
Oregon State Alignment

In service of the state’s future

Unlocking district-scale economic growth that individual projects cannot deliver.

  • A coordinated urban development initiative that strengthens housing supply, job creation, and long-term tax base growth at once
  • A phased, infrastructure-aligned approach supporting housing delivery, workforce stability, and private investment confidence
  • Designed to align with statewide housing production goals, economic development priorities, and talent retention strategies
  • Advances efficient use of Opportunity Zone and urban renewal tools without expanding public balance-sheet risk
  • Supports construction, small business formation, and innovation-sector employment across the Willamette Valley
  • A replicable district framework for mid-sized Oregon cities seeking durable, market-driven growth
These commitments carry through the whole plan - the impact & placemaking framework below, the activation strategy that opens the site to the community years before vertical construction, and the phasing discipline that ties growth to real absorption.
Impact & Placemaking

Designed for everyday vitality

Now City's mission is to accelerate the transition to regenerative, resilient, and equitable communities and cities.

Regeneration

Restore the natural systems that sustain daily life (clean air, water, soil, and food), improving personal health while rebuilding ecological function at the neighborhood and district scale.

Resilience

Homes, mobility, energy, food, and social systems are designed to perform under stress (climate and economic disruption), strengthening household security and community-wide stability.

Equity

Designing around the realities of caregiving, childhood, aging, mobility independence, and access to opportunity improves daily life for individuals while creating more stable, connected, and inclusive communities for all.

Regenerative Placemaking

  • Built for Well-Being - biophilic streetscapes, shaded walking paths, green plazas, and active gathering spaces that support daily movement, mental health, and social connection
  • Culture & Flavor - breweries, food halls, artisan retail, and year-round programming that celebrate local creativity, craft, and regional identity
  • Innovation in Daily Life - maker spaces, startup studios, and flexible work environments that place entrepreneurship, research, and creation directly into the fabric of the district
  • Connected Living - walkable streets, safe bike networks, and future-forward transit connections that seamlessly link the district to downtown Salem, the riverfront, and the region

Resilience by Design

Now City West Salem is designed to quietly become a resilience hub - not through emergency labeling, but through everyday civic life. By anchoring the district in shared gathering, local infrastructure, and walkable access to daily needs, resilience emerges naturally as a byproduct of good urban design.

  1. Sports and event spaces designed to serve double duty as community response anchors
  2. District-scale green infrastructure integrating power, heating and cooling, wastewater, last-mile mobility, and data infrastructure for continuous operation under stress
  3. High-paying innovation jobs + regenerative placemaking connecting people to nature, heritage, and long-term opportunity

Beyond Agrihoods: Food as District Infrastructure

A living innovation framework to design, test, and scale urban+rural circular food systems. By linking regenerative agriculture, food logistics, local markets, waste recovery, and community participation, the district becomes a real-world platform for proving what truly resilient food economies look like.

  1. Urban+rural circular food systems connecting regional regenerative farms with on-site processing, markets, and daily life
  2. Living testbed for agri-tech + soil + food innovation supporting founders, researchers, and farmers
  3. Closed-loop nutrient and organic waste recovery feeding back into regional food production
  4. Scalable agrihood innovation framework that can be replicated globally

Regenerative AI - A New Kind of Innovation District

At first glance, "Regenerative AI" may sound like an oxymoron. We believe it reflects the work ahead: redirecting powerful digital tools toward the healing and long-term resilience of living systems - incubating a new generation of graduates, founders, and community leaders applying AI directly within the fabric of daily life.

  1. Improve building performance, energy efficiency, and material stewardship
  2. Support mobility optimization and accessibility
  3. Enable real-time water, waste, and resource intelligence
  4. Strengthen food systems, climate adaptation, and carbon awareness
  5. Enhance public health insights, safety, and community services

Landscape Intelligence: Designing Nature as Infrastructure

Not just parks or green decoration - treating landscape as living infrastructure: integrated ecology, climate adaptation, social systems, and public health. Designing with nature's logic, not tacking nature onto development.

  1. "City-Nature" as foundational infrastructure - ecology woven into streets, plazas, and open space as a working system
  2. Climate and water performance by design - cooling, stormwater, and microclimate regulation through living landscapes
  3. Health, biodiversity, and public life integrated - landscapes that support well-being, habitat, and social connection at once
  4. Adaptive and evolving open space - flexible landscapes that change with use, seasons, and the district's growth

Women-First, Family-First, and Aging-In-Place Design

Communities designed around the daily realities of women, children, and aging adults consistently outperform conventional development on health, safety, stability, and long-term value. A women-first lens creates places that work better for everyone.

  1. Aging-in-place housing + services supporting independence, dignity, and reduced healthcare burden over time
  2. Child-centered public realm with safe mobility, nature-based play, and everyday supervision through active streets
  3. Women-first safety, access, and care design shown to increase walkability, retail vitality, and neighborhood stability
  4. Intergenerational community fabric proven to reduce isolation, improve mental health, and increase long-term residency
The Work Itself

What each question could take to answer

Everything on this page is an argument, and arguments are easy to make. Each of these should be commissioned independently, scoped in public, and published in full whichever way it comes out.

Work

The regional economic base and where it could grow: who employs whom now, how much of the advanced-industry workforce commutes in from outside, what small businesses need that they are missing, and what a shared fabrication and testing capacity would be worth to the companies already here. Including a jobs estimate built from a published method rather than asserted.

Movement

What the corridor costs today: commute times and their effect on hiring, freight economics on the existing rail line, the labor market that four cities would share if the distance behaved differently, and which interventions would change it at what price.

Homes

What housing the market and local incomes actually support across income bands, what the delivery capacity is against the state's production targets, and whether building components made in the region could close part of the cost gap.

The ground itself

Site and district feasibility across the study area: title, environmental, geotechnical, utilities, stormwater, transportation and the entitlement pathway. Findings sorted into no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or potential fatal flaw. This is the piece that lets the other three be tested somewhere real.

The question about traffic, daily life and local ecology should be measured inside this scope: vehicle trips per household against Salem averages, land consumed per home, and the difference between adapting a building and replacing it. If the argument holds, this is the basis for a district and a substantial amount of housing. If it fails, Salem learns why at low cost, and so does every other mid-size city working on the same problem.

What Is Needed

What is needed now

Public and institutional sponsors to hold and commission the work.

Now City is not an eligible applicant for most of the funding available for work of this kind, and that is appropriate. Public money should not pay a private developer to study its own site. What is needed are partners who can hold the work, commission it independently and publish it: cities, counties, economic development organizations, universities and foundations. The questions can be split between several sponsors, which is usually easier than finding one body to carry all of them.

What it produces is a public asset. Independent, published answers about work, movement and housing in this region, available to every developer, employer and public body that comes afterward, whatever they conclude about us. It is also the thing that lets private capital price Salem, which is the honest reason we want it done.
