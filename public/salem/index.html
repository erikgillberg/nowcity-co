A Proposal for Study

How might Salem become the most livable city in America by 2050?

A district on the Willamette in Salem, and the studies that would test whether it works.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
In Short

A riverfront district where people can live, build and belong

Designed to make Salem a place where talented people come to build things and stay to build a life. The reasoning runs in six steps, and every one of them is worth arguing with.

01

Why Oregon?

Oregon already has the ingredients: a semiconductor cluster, a robotics research base, one of the most productive farm valleys in the country, a mass timber sector and a health system, all within about a hundred miles of each other.

02

Why physical AI?

Several of Oregon's strongest industries increasingly draw on the same engineering, and a robotics manufacturer has already built its factory in Salem.

03

Why an Innovation District?

Those companies need physical places to prototype, manufacture, collaborate and grow, close enough together to share what none of them would buy alone.

04

Why housing and urbanism?

Companies compete for people, and people choose places. A district that is good to live in is part of how the jobs get filled.

05

Why Salem?

Affordability, the state capital and its institutions, an existing industrial base, a riverfront, a position in the middle of the corridor, and room to grow.

06

Why now?

The companies are arriving before the place around them has been built on purpose. Deciding what goes on this ground is easier before the ground is spoken for.

What happens next. Treat all of the above as a hypothesis and test it. The study plan sets out the six questions that would settle it, the order they would run in, and who is best placed to run each one.
Who We Are

Why we are writing this down

Now City is a development company. We want to build in Salem, and we would like other private capital to invest here alongside us.

We plan and build districts: the homes, the workspace and the public ground between them, on sites that are already inside a city rather than at its edge. We are a small company, we have not built in Salem yet, and this page is the first time we have set out publicly what we think this city could do. Everything on this site is a proposal, and every image of the district is a concept render.

Private capital does not arrive in a place until some basic questions have been answered. We went looking for current published answers to several of them and could not find them. What the regional economy could support. What the distance between the valley's cities costs the people and employers along it. What kind of housing local incomes actually sustain, and who could build it at the pace the state now requires.

Without those answers a developer has to build the evidence from scratch for every project, which is slow and expensive. So rather than assemble it privately, we are proposing the questions be studied openly, by people with no stake in the outcome, and published in full. If the answers are encouraging we build, and so can others. If they are discouraging, everybody finds out cheaply. And if some of this work already exists, we would be glad to be pointed at it.

The Test

How might this region grow in a way that improves lives and ecosystems?

Salem has about 183,000 residents and has been adding people steadily for decades. It will keep growing whether or not anyone plans for it. Growth by itself does nothing for livability, and in most American cities it works against it: more cars, longer trips, more farmland consumed, more runoff into the river. That outcome follows from where the homes go rather than from how many people arrive.

Traffic

Households in walkable, mixed-use areas make fewer and shorter vehicle trips than households at the edge of a city. Homes placed near work, schools, shops and transit reduce total driving even as the population rises.

Daily life

Living close to things gives people time back. A shorter commute, a school within walking distance and a market on the way home change how a day is spent more than any single amenity does.

Local ecology

Reusing buildings that already stand consumes no new land, and compact development leaves farmland and habitat intact. At district scale, stormwater can be cleaned before it reaches the river, the bank can be replanted, and canopy and habitat added rather than removed.

Say Salem added thirty thousand residents over the next twenty years. That is close to its existing trend line, so the number is not the difficult part. Whether those homes go at the edge or in the middle decides whether the city that results is better to live in than the one we have now. That is what a district like this is meant to test, and the study plan sets out how it would be measured.

What Needs Answering

Three questions underneath it

Each of these is a public question rather than a private one. Each has a natural sponsor better placed to answer it than we are. And each is one the region would benefit from having a published answer to.

Work

How might Salem and Oregon support small business and build on the physical AI work already here?

The valley already holds the pieces: a semiconductor cluster to the north, a robotics research group at Oregon State, one of the most diverse farm valleys in the country, a health system, a mass-timber sector, and a humanoid robot factory in Salem. What it lacks is a place where they meet and a path for the small companies that grow up around them.

Natural sponsors: Business Oregon, the regional economic development organization, the universities and colleges, employers. The long version.

Mobility

How might the friction along the Portland, Salem, Corvallis and Eugene corridor be reduced?

Four cities inside about 110 miles that behave as four separate labor markets. Someone living in Corvallis and a job in Hillsboro are two hours apart in practice. That gap is a real cost to employers and households, and we could not find a published estimate of its size.

Natural sponsors: ODOT, the councils of government, the corridor cities. The rail corridor and district mobility.

Homes

How might Salem and the region meet the housing shortage?

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions, so the strategy exists. Delivery capacity is the open question, and so is which housing types local incomes actually support.

Natural sponsors: Oregon Housing and Community Services, DLCD, the City, philanthropy. The long version.

Jobs, travel and housing are usually studied separately, so nobody sees how they affect one another. A district puts all three on the same ground at the same time, which makes them possible to observe together. That is the reason to build one.
The Vision

What this could look like on the ground

The three questions above meet in one place: a district on both banks of the Willamette, built largely inside buildings that already stand. Two pages carry the full picture.

Every district image on this site is a concept render. No land has been acquired for the wider district, no partner has been committed to it, and the drawings are there to make the idea specific enough to discuss.

What Happens Next

The studies that would decide this

A drawing of a district costs very little and proves nothing. What decides whether anything gets built are the studies behind it: what the market supports, what the ground can carry, what the public sector would do, and what housing costs allow. As far as we can tell, most of those are still open for this part of Salem.

01

Commission the questions independently

Scoped in public, carried out by people with no stake in the answer, and published in full whichever way they come out. A study that shows the first phase should be smaller is a useful study.

02

Split them between sponsors

Work, mobility and homes have different natural holders, and the questions divide cleanly. That is usually easier than finding one body willing to carry all of them.

03

Publish the answers as a regional asset

Whatever the studies conclude about us, the findings stay available to every developer, employer and public body that comes afterward.

The first step is a meeting.

Now City is an eligible applicant for almost none of the funding available for studies of this kind, and that is appropriate. Public money should not pay a private developer to study its own site. What is needed are sponsors who can commission the studies independently and publish them: cities, counties, economic development organizations, universities and foundations.

The study plan sets out the six questions, the order they would run in, and who is best placed to run each one.
