A Proposal for Study

How might Salem become the most livable city in America by 2050?

A district on the Willamette River in Salem, and the studies that would test whether it works.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
Now City in Salem

A riverfront district where people can live, build and belong

Designed to make Salem a place where talented people come to build things and stay to build a life.

Now City is a development company. We want to build in Salem, and we would like other private capital to invest here alongside us.

We seek to engage a coalition of local support to test the assumptions behind the bright future we see for Salem.

The Problem

Why this is worth doing now

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025, with seventeen actions aimed at getting more homes built. Wherever those homes go, the choice is the same one: the edge of the city, where streets, water, sewer and services have to be built and then maintained, or land inside the city that is already served. That choice is being made now, one project at a time.

Oregon is short roughly 15,500 homes a year against its own published production target of 29,522, which is our arithmetic from the state’s own numbers rather than an agency figure (how that is calculated). An analysis of Census data released in January shows the state losing residents aged thirty to fifty faster than it gains them, with housing cost among the causes, while the people arriving earn considerably less than the people leaving (Oregon Journalism Project, March 2026). Those two things meet at the price of a house. A household that grew up in Salem competes for the same homes as a household arriving on a Portland or California wage, and the people Salem trains often take work elsewhere.

The constraint here is delivery. Oregon has already settled one part of this: one plan approval and a state insignia valid in every Oregon jurisdiction. We went looking for a state program that markets this to builders and could not find one, and would be glad to be pointed at it. What binds instead is factory throughput, transport distance, construction finance and the duration of the capital. No single builder fixes those alone, which is one of the reasons to work at district scale rather than site by site. Housing supply, in detail.
The Starting Position

What Salem already has

These conditions are in place today and are mostly under-used.

A river through the middle

The Willamette runs between downtown and West Salem, with public land, an island park and two bridges. Most of the frontage is currently industrial or fenced.

Capital city institutions

State government, a regional hospital system, universities within reach, and community colleges running programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics. Employment here is steadier through downturns than in comparable cities.

A position on the corridor

Salem is within an hour of Portland, the Hillsboro semiconductor cluster, Oregon State and Eugene. It is the only point on that line within reach of all four, and the seat of state government.

An existing industrial base

Food processing, wood products, logistics, advanced manufacturing and a humanoid robotics factory. The jobs span several wage levels.

Land and relative affordability

Developable land inside the urban growth boundary, industrial buildings suitable for adaptation, and prices that still allow ownership on local wages.

Supportive adopted policy

Our Salem calls for mixed-use riverfront development and housing near major employment centers. The City’s 2026-2031 Strategic Plan puts the City in the role of Partner on lowering the cost of multi-family housing. Its Climate Action Plan strategy list, which is advisory, recommends housing near employment and walkable mixed-use close to downtown.

The Brookings Metro Monitor for March 2026 places Salem fourth for prosperity and seventh for growth among 85 US metro areas of between 250,000 and 500,000 people, and in the top fifth of all metro areas for inclusion. The figures reach us through a stakeholder submission to the Oregon Prosperity Council rather than through the Council’s own findings.

Conditions Today

What a district here would need to change

These are five conditions on the ground today. Each takes longer than one project to change, which is part of why a district rather than a site.

01

Walkable density outside a few downtown blocks

Most of the city is built at a scale that requires a car for every trip, which limits street life and raises household transport costs.

02

A working riverfront

There is no continuous public edge along the water, and few places to eat, work, live or launch a boat.

03

Housing delivery at the pace the state now requires

Salem adopted its first Housing Production Strategy in 2025 with seventeen actions. The open question is delivery capacity.

04

More than one employer to move for

A company looks for a labor market before it relocates. An engineer looks for more than one employer before doing the same, because a single job is a single point of failure. Each waits on the other, and Salem keeps educating people who take their skills to Portland or further.

05

A destination on each side of the river

West Salem is largely residential and downtown empties in the evening, so the bridges carry commuters in the morning and again at five.

The Proposition

A district built around the river

Salem has a river through the middle of it, and very little built to face the water. The area under study runs along both sides of it, because a continuous public edge, a crossing built for people and shared infrastructure only make sense at that scale. An old railroad bridge already links the two sides and carries people on foot and on bikes.

At the bridges

The downtown waterfront on one side and the industrial blocks on the other, treated as one place rather than two. The west bank has buildings worth adapting for workspace and homes above. The east bank has open waterfront that would take new construction. A market and a continuous public edge along the water.

Downstream

A large area of riverfront land, most of it undeveloped, which gives the work room to continue once the first phases are complete.

What aggregation makes possible

A public realm that runs through

The greenway, the river promenade, the market square and the crossing work at the length of the whole place. One plan over contiguous land is what draws them as one continuous thing and holds the alignment as each phase is built.

A street network you can walk

Block sizes, connections and where the through routes go are decisions that have to be taken across a whole area at once.

Infrastructure and repetition

Streets, stormwater, utilities and shared parking can be sized once for the area and paid for across it. A small set of building types repeated across phases is also the condition under which offsite manufacturing pays. Whether either lowers the cost of a home here is something the studies would price.

A district also has time to fill, and the standing buildings on the west bank are what fill it. Space that is vacant or under-used can carry markets, food, events and maker space long before any new construction finishes, so a market, a food operator and a workshop tenant can be in the ground while the rest is still being studied.

Work, On The Ground

The work the district could hold

The employment here could span several wage levels. Fabrication, food production, maker space and light industry, research and engineering. That range matters, because a district has to house the people who work in it.

Large-format industrial development belongs at Mill Creek Corporate Center on the east side of the city, which is already built and marketed for it. This district holds the smaller-format work that fits beside housing: workshops, assembly, studios and labs in buildings that already stand.

Make building components

Oregon has a mass-timber sector, a federal Tech Hub for it, and an established wood products industry in the valley. The regional housing shortage is the demand.

Retain graduates

Community college and university programs in robotics, machining and industrial electronics already operate here. What is required is local work and housing within reach of it.

House the workforce

Employment growth on the east side of the city creates demand for housing. This district is within reach of it, and the market study would establish how much of that demand it could serve.

Living Here

How the homes and blocks could work

The blocks are drawn around a shared garden, with shops and daily life at the street edge. Homes face the street on one side and the garden on the other, which gives most of them daylight from two directions. The mix runs from studios to three-bedroom townhomes, for renting or for owning.

A garden in the middle

Each block wraps a shared garden that children can use, overlooked by the homes around it, so several families can see the same space from their kitchens.

Shops and workspace at the street

Ground floors hold shops, cafes and workspace, so daily errands can be done on foot and the street stays in use through the day.

Homes of several sizes

Studios through three-bedroom townhomes in the same block, built to a standard that keeps energy bills low, so households at different stages can stay in one neighborhood.

Inside the Home

How the homes are built

The homes use wood and other natural materials, with insulation, windows and ventilation built to a standard that holds temperature and keeps running costs down.

The Ground Floor

What the ground floors hold

The ground floors hold the things a household needs most days: childcare, a clinic, food, and space small businesses can afford. Putting them in the block means those trips are made on foot rather than by car.

Landmarks & Mobility

Two ideas worth testing

Two of the ideas on this page are less settled than the rest. Both would need their own study, and the district would work without either. They are here because they are worth arguing about now rather than later.

The Landmark

The Salem Forest Tower

A timber tower with a continuous ramp to the top, so it can be climbed by anyone, including people using a wheelchair or a stroller. The climb would be used to tell the story of the place: the people who lived here first, the valley’s farming and biodiversity, and the river itself. From the top you would see the Willamette running north and south.

  • Precedent: EFFEKT's Camp Adventure Forest Tower in Denmark - a 45 m observation tower with a 900 m treetop boardwalk, climbable by a continuous ramp, engineered with ARUP and opened to the public in March 2019
  • It is described as one of the most visited attractions in Denmark. We went looking for published visitor numbers and payback figures and could not find them from a primary source, so establishing what a landmark of this kind actually draws and costs is question 09 in the study plan
  • Whether a landmark here would draw visitors, what it would cost to build and run, and what it would do for nearby retail and lodging are the questions the visitor destination study would answer

Photograph: Camp Adventure Forest Tower, Denmark, by EFFEKT with ARUP, opened 2019. Shown as the precedent described above rather than as a design for Salem.

The Mobility System

A cable circulator, one candidate technology

Swyft Cities' Whoosh is a cable-based network of five-passenger, battery-electric autonomous vehicles - 30 mph, with offline stations so every trip is nonstop and a vehicle is always waiting. It runs above Highway 22, the Willamette, Pringle Creek and the rail line, which are the things that make crossing this part of Salem slow. A concept sketch for Salem maps 16 stations across 4.5 route miles linking West Salem, downtown, Riverfront Park, and Willamette University.

  • At Irvine's 1,300-acre Great Park, the City's independent travel demand forecast projects 27,000 daily riders - with 4x the ridership of a traditional circulator at one-sixth the operating cost per trip
  • Irvine City Council selected Whoosh as the Great Park’s primary transportation technology in April 2025. The largest Whoosh built so far is a quarter-scale demonstration
  • Salem’s Climate Action Plan strategy list recommends a feasibility study on an electric downtown circulator, including a West Salem connection, with Cherriots as lead agency. Whoosh is one candidate technology that study could weigh

Render by Swyft Cities. Source: "The Potential for a Whoosh Transit Circulator in Salem, OR" (Now City & partners, 2026) and the City of Irvine Great Park travel demand analysis cited therein.

Both are early concepts. Neither has been reviewed or agreed by any public agency, and the district would work without either of them.
For Salem & Oregon

Who this is being designed for

The district is being designed for the families, workers and businesses who live in the Willamette Valley now. The measurements on this page and in the study plan are how that gets checked rather than asserted.

What is around Salem

Salem sits in the middle of the Willamette Valley, which is one of the most productive farming regions in the country. The coast and the Cascades are both about an hour away by road, and the state capital and its institutions are here.

45 minutes to Portland - international airport, talent, culture - and 60 minutes to the Oregon Coast.

People arrive here from the more expensive West Coast metros. Oregon ranked first in the nation for inbound share of moves in 2025, and Salem seventh among US metros, on one moving company’s own shipment data (United Van Lines). That counts arrivals rather than net migration, and Oregon is still losing residents aged thirty to fifty, which is the problem set out at the top of this page.

Housing costs less here than in the metros people arrive from. Closing that gap fairly, for the households already living in Salem, is what the outcomes on this page are meant to test.

City of Salem Alignment

Where this would need to line up with the city

Places where a district on this ground would meet work the City has already adopted or has under way this year.

  • Sits alongside the Center Street Bridge project and the water transmission line to West Salem, both of which cross this ground
  • Depends on the Water and Wastewater Utility Master Plans the City is refreshing this year, which would set what this area can carry
  • Would have to fit the North Waterfront urban renewal plan the City is taking up this year. We went looking for the boundary and could not find a published map
  • Adds to the tax base over time, while the industrial uses on the west bank carry on until they choose to move
  • Adds homes priced against what people in Salem actually earn
  • Opens the riverfront to the public and makes the crossing between the two banks work for people on foot and on bikes
  • Puts homes near daily needs, which reduces what the city has to build and then maintain
  • Builds in phases, so density and traffic arrive at the pace the market and the streets can take
  • Keeps growth that is coming anyway inside the city rather than at its edge
State of Oregon Alignment

Where it would line up with the state

Six places where a district on this ground would meet goals the State has already set.

  • Works on homes, jobs and the tax base at the same time rather than one at a time
  • Builds in phases, so the infrastructure and the homes arrive together
  • Adds homes toward the production target the state has already set
  • Uses urban renewal and Opportunity Zone tools that already exist, rather than asking for new ones
  • Puts construction work, small business space and technical jobs in the same place
  • If it works, other mid-size Oregon cities could copy it. If it fails, they learn why at our expense
None of this has been agreed with anyone. It is our reading of where a district on this ground would have to line up with plans the City and the State have already adopted, and it is one of the things the study plan would establish rather than assume.
What The District Would Try To Do

What we would design for

These are the things Now City would design for here. Each one can be checked by a study or a measurement, and several line up with the outcome measures the City proposes in its 2026-2031 Strategic Plan.

Clean air, water and soil

Stormwater cleaned on the block before it reaches the river. The bank replanted. Tree canopy and habitat added rather than removed. The City’s 2026-2031 Strategic Plan proposes to measure water quality, riparian acres restored and tree canopy coverage, so these could be reported the same way.

Homes that hold up under stress

Buildings, energy, water and food supply designed to keep working through a heat wave, a flood or a power outage, so households are safer and the wider city has somewhere that still functions.

A place that works for everyone in a household

Designed around caregiving, small children, older residents and people who do not drive. The measures are daylight, walking distance, step-free access, and somewhere to sit along the way.

Public space and daily life

The parts of a neighborhood people use every day, put close enough together that a day takes fewer car trips.

  • Streets built for walking with shade, planting and places to sit, so short trips get made on foot
  • Food and drink at ground level, breweries, a food hall, small shops and year-round programming
  • Workshops and studios in the same blocks as homes, so making things stays visible in daily life
  • Safe bike routes and a transit connection to downtown Salem, the riverfront and the wider region

A district that keeps working in an emergency

Salem’s Climate Action Plan strategy list recommends a network of neighborhood resilience hubs: indoor places that work as community centers most of the time, and as cooling centers, food distribution points, places to charge during an outage and evacuation sites when they are needed. A district can hold one without building anything for that purpose alone.

  1. Sports halls and event spaces that can serve as gathering points when the city needs them
  2. District-scale power, heating, cooling and water systems that keep running when the wider network is under strain
  3. Homes within walking distance of daily needs, so a disruption to driving does not cut people off from food and care

Food, from the valley to the block

The Willamette Valley is one of the most diverse farming regions in the country, and Salem sits in the middle of it. A district here could connect that farming to processing, markets and daily meals in one place.

  1. Regional farms connected to on-site processing and markets, shortening the distance between a field and a kitchen
  2. Space for food and soil businesses to test things, for growers, researchers and small producers
  3. Food waste composted and returned to regional farms rather than sent to landfill
  4. A model other towns in the valley could copy if it works here

Where new technology would be put to work

Oregon already has companies and researchers building tools that act on the physical world. A district gives them somewhere real to apply that work, on problems people can see. These are the five we would start with.

  1. Making buildings use less energy and fewer materials
  2. Making it easier to get around without a car
  3. Measuring water use, waste and energy so the district can see how it is doing
  4. Supporting food production and tracking the district's carbon
  5. Improving public health services, safety and everyday care

Landscape doing real work

Planting here would be built to perform, in the same way a street or a pipe performs. It cools the air, holds and cleans water, and supports habitat, at the same time as being somewhere pleasant to sit.

  1. Planting integrated into streets, plazas and open space as part of how the district works
  2. Cooling and stormwater handled by the landscape, which reduces what the city has to build and maintain
  3. Habitat and public space on the same ground, along the bank and through the blocks
  4. Open space that can change with the seasons and as the district grows

Designed around women, children and older residents

Places designed around the daily realities of women, children and older adults tend to do better on health, safety and how long people stay. Designing for the people with the least margin produces a place that works for everyone.

  1. Homes and services that let people age in place, which keeps independence longer and costs the health system less
  2. A public realm children can use, with safe routes, places to play and enough activity on the street that they are watched
  3. Safety, access and care designed in from the start, which also tends to improve walking and local trade
  4. Households of different ages in the same blocks, which reduces isolation and keeps people living here longer
