The Plan

What needs to happen next

The questions that would decide whether a district on the Willamette works, who is best placed to answer each one, and what we are asking for.

Now City · Salem, Oregon · September 2026
Why Study First

The vision is an argument, and arguments are cheap

A drawing of a district costs very little and proves nothing. What decides whether anything gets built is a much drier body of work: what the market supports, what the ground can carry, what the public sector would do, and what the housing math allows. In Salem most of that work has not been done, so every project here is underwritten as though it were the first one.

The proposal on this page is to answer those questions properly and in public, before anyone commits capital. Commissioned independently, scoped so that the findings belong to the region rather than to us, and published in full whichever way they come out. That last part matters. A study that can only confirm the sponsor's hopes is worth nothing to a lender, and everyone in the room knows it.

Findings that shrink the first phase are still findings. A first phase materially smaller than the vision is a good outcome if it is the one the evidence supports, because it is the one that gets financed.

The Questions

Six questions the work would answer

These are the questions a serious lender, a public body or a family office asks before committing to anything at district scale. They can be split between several sponsors, which is usually easier than finding one body willing to carry all of them.

01 · What the market supports

Two questions rather than one. What can a first phase be underwritten against on today's evidence, using existing comparable buildings and nothing else? And separately, what conditions would have to become true for the ground to support materially more? Conflating those two is how ambitious districts quietly become conventional ones.

02 · Whether the ground can carry it

Title, environmental, civil, geotechnical, utilities, flood and stormwater, transportation and easements across the study area. Findings sorted into three groups: no material issue, solvable at a known cost, or a potential fatal flaw. Capital tolerates identified risk with a price attached. It will not tolerate an unexamined site.

03 · What should actually be built

Turning the vision into a framework: streets, blocks, parks, infrastructure and development parcels, then a first phase defined precisely enough to price. Big vision, small first bite. The ground holds optionality, and holding optionality means most of it stays undeveloped for a long time.

04 · What the public sector would do

Beyond general statements of support, the specific tools: urban renewal and tax increment, infrastructure participation, system development charge treatment, the permitting pathway, housing incentives and workforce programs. And a written record of which party is responsible for each infrastructure item.

05 · What private capital would need to see

At the end of the work, the next capital requirement and its uses should be legible: validated market assumptions, priced site risks, documented public participation, a land strategy, an entitlement budget, first phase economics, and the milestones the money would clear. That turns a concept into an opportunity with a known shape.

06 · How many jobs, and how the number is built

The first question anyone asks, and the one most often answered badly. A number produced before the study is a number produced by whoever wants it to be large. The method has to be published alongside the figure.

How The Answers Would Be Built

A ladder of cases, each with its conditions

A single predicted number is the least useful output a study of this kind can produce, because it hides the thing everyone actually needs to see: what separates a modest outcome from an ambitious one, and who controls each step between them.

01
Floor case

What existing Salem comparables support today, with no district effect assumed. This is the rung the first phase is financed against, and it is deliberately conservative.

02
Base case

The floor plus whatever premium comparable districts elsewhere have demonstrably achieved, benchmarked against those places rather than against Salem's current stock.

03
Conditional cases

Two or three coherent futures, each with named trigger conditions and dates. An employer arriving. A transportation decision. A housing program reaching scale. Each rung says who controls the condition and by when it would have to happen.

Four methods produce this without giving up rigor, and each one is standard practice a reviewer will recognise. Threshold analysis runs the model backwards, solving for the rent, absorption or employment growth a project requires to clear its hurdle, then asking how plausible that is. Backcasting starts at the intended district and works back to the decisions required to reach it, with dates and owners attached. What would have to be true lists the conditions that would make the district the right choice and tests the least likely one first. Employer-anchored demand counts the people who work in the area and live elsewhere, rather than only the households already shopping the local market.

One addition worth making explicit. Revealed preference cannot see a product that does not exist locally. Where the study asks about housing types Salem has never been offered, it should use stated-preference research, which is the accepted method for exactly that blind spot. Otherwise the study measures the existing market and calls it the ceiling.
What Would Be Measured

Three numbers that make the claim falsifiable

The argument for a district like this is that a region can add people while daily life and local ecology improve. That is a testable claim, and these are the measurements that test it.

Vehicle trips per household

Measured against Salem averages. If households in a compact riverfront district drive as much as households at the edge, the central premise fails and everyone should know that.

Land consumed per home

Acres per dwelling, compared with what the same number of homes would consume at the city's edge. This is where farmland, habitat and the cost of new infrastructure are actually decided.

Carbon in adaptation against replacement

The difference between reusing a building that already stands and putting up a new one in its place, counted honestly, including what the reuse costs in energy performance.

If the argument holds, this becomes the basis for a district and a substantial amount of housing. If it fails, Salem learns why at low cost, and so does every other mid-size city working on the same problem. Both outcomes are worth the price of the work.

Constraints We Accept

Two things this proposal will not ask Salem for

Salem's adopted plan already contains most of what we are describing. Working inside it is both easier and more honest than asking the city to adopt something new, and it means accepting two limits up front.

High-value industrial land stays industrial

Salem's comprehensive plan directs the city to preserve large, flat, serviced industrial sites with highway access. The employment land east of the city is exactly that, and it should keep doing the job it was set aside for. Nothing in this proposal asks for its conversion.

The industrial land surplus gets stated plainly

Salem's economic opportunities analysis found a commercial deficit alongside an industrial surplus. Any honest study has to work from that finding rather than around it, and it is one of the reasons the case here rests on housing, mixed use and the riverfront rather than on new industrial land.

There is a live question inside the second point. That analysis was adopted in 2015 for a twenty year horizon and is now eleven years old, and industrial land has been consumed since. Whether the surplus still exists at the size the document reports is itself worth checking, and it is the kind of thing a study can settle in weeks.

The Order

What would run when

The sequence matters because the questions gate one another, and because doing them in the wrong order is how study money gets spent twice.

01
The market question and the ground, in parallel

These two are independent of each other and everything else waits on both. The market work sets what can be financed. The site work says whether there is anything to finance it on. Neither takes long, and running them together saves a season.

02
The public conversation, starting at the same time

What the city, the region and the state would actually do is a conversation rather than a report, so it needs the longest runway. Beginning it alongside the technical work means the answers arrive together rather than a year apart.

03
The framework and the first phase

Once the market and the ground have answered, the district framework and a precisely defined first phase can be drawn against real constraints rather than against hope.

04
Economic and fiscal impact, last

The jobs and revenue numbers depend on what the framework says gets built, so producing them earlier produces a number with no method behind it. This is also where the corridor questions about movement and housing capacity get folded back in.

One practical warning for any sponsor. Most public funding programs disallow costs incurred before an award is made. Work that starts early, however sensible, is usually work that cannot be counted afterwards. Any sequencing decision should be checked against that rule before a single engagement is signed.
Division of Labor

Who does what

Most of the work described here belongs to other parties. This is our understanding of how it would divide.

WhoWhat only they can do
The CityLand use, the entitlement pathway, urban renewal financing, the public realm, and the decision about the future of the riverfront.
Region and StateSite readiness, transportation, workforce programs, and funding routes that a private applicant cannot access.
EmployersStating what they need in order to hire and retain people here, and whether workforce housing nearby would affect their decisions.
Universities and collegesResearch capacity, the training pipeline, and shared instrumentation that no single company would buy alone.
The communityA seat in governance from the start, and the standing to say when the work is going wrong.
PhilanthropyFunding the studies before there is a commercial answer to sell.
Now CityAssembling the question, carrying development risk, and building a first phase that makes the rest credible.
What Is Needed

What we are asking for

Public and institutional sponsors to hold and commission the work.

Now City is an eligible applicant for almost none of the funding available for work of this kind, and that is appropriate. Public money should not pay a private developer to study its own site. What is needed are partners who can hold the work, commission it independently and publish it: cities, counties, economic development organizations, universities and foundations. The questions divide cleanly, so no single body has to carry all of them.

The most useful first step is convening rather than funding. A room with the city, the region, the state, the employers and the institutions in it, agreeing on which questions matter and who holds each one, is worth more at this stage than any single grant.

What it produces is a public asset. Independent, published answers about work, movement and housing in this region, available to every developer, employer and public body that comes afterward, whatever they conclude about us. It is also the thing that lets private capital price Salem, which is the honest reason we want it done.
