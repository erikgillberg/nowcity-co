1Part I · The Constraint

What would it take to build and operate one million homes this way over thirty years?

The million is a diagnostic, not a target. We use the number to find out what the business model has to become, because the answer disqualifies the business almost everyone in this industry is in.

As a boast, it invites dismissal from anyone who knows the ground truth. As a stress test, it is the most useful question in the plan.

So the question is which engine carries volume and which carries margin. This is not a scaling problem; it is a structural ceiling on the development business model.

6Part II · The Compounding Engine

Why the opportunity exists: fragmentation

Construction, infrastructure, capital, and operations are each optimized separately, by different firms, on different clocks, with different exit horizons. Cross-layer gains are structurally unclaimable by anyone optimizing one layer.

The concrete case: a Passive House envelope raises capex and lowers operating cost for decades. A merchant builder cannot capture that, because they sell before the operating years arrive, so they value-engineer it out. Every rational actor in the current structure makes the same choice. The gain is not hard to find. It is unownable.

Now City holds. That single fact makes the trade available. The same logic runs through district energy, water reuse, mobility, and ground-floor programming. This is the technical claim the whole company rests on: checkable, not a matter of taste, and independent of anyone believing in regenerative development.

8Part II · The Rate

The rate, stated honestly

We do not have a rate. At n=1, nobody could. What we have is a mechanism and an instrument, and the specific purpose of districts two and three is to produce the first two data points.

Anyone who gives you a learning-curve percentage before their second project is making it up. Naming the rate is the deliverable of Phase 2, not a claim of Phase 1.

9Part II · Precedent

Precedent: borrow the mechanism, choose the anchor

Four moves worth borrowing, all from the same playbook: co-design the whole stack rather than one layer; partner on manufacturing but never on product definition; keep improving the product after delivery; and let the standard spread further than your own production.

AnchorMechanism it demonstrates
Tesla (US audiences)The full sequence: a staged master plan funding each phase from the last; one platform expanded into an adjacent product line (cars → energy storage → solar → compute); manufacturing as the product ("the machine that builds the machine"); fleet data compounding into the software advantage; and sibling ventures (SpaceX) sharing talent, tooling discipline, and credibility without sharing a balance sheet. Now City's analog: district → catalog → infrastructure → operations, with the operating data as the fleet.
Kombohus (institutional / Nordic)Demand aggregation producing real factory economics: ~10,000 apartments, ~5 months faster, 20-25% below comparable cost (Sveriges Allmännytta). Proven at national scale, and already in our materials.
LEGOOne standardized system, unlimited contextual expression, sustained for generations.
ØrstedInfrastructure ownership and a long-horizon transformation, with the volatility that comes with it.

Lead with the mechanism; let the reader supply the comparison. The American anchor is most legible in the US and most contested in Europe; default to Nordic anchors for institutional audiences.

10Part II · The Plan

The plan, in four sentences

1Build one district as one product, at a cost that only makes sense as proof.
2Use it to build a small number of districts and finish the catalog.
3Use the catalog to make the chassis the thing others default to.
4And operate every district, forever, so each one teaches the next.

17Part IV · Stewardship

Say this plainly: stewardship is currently intention plus advisory relationships. The instruments that would make it binding do not exist. Naming that is what earns the institutional reader; claiming otherwise is what loses them permanently.

20Part V · Acquisitions

The acquisition roadmap: buy capabilities on the way to the vision

Precedent: Greystar reached ~1M units under management by 2026 from a 1993 founding, substantially through acquisitions and portfolio partnerships, including a 2025 partnership adding 11,000 units in one move (NMHC 2026; Multifamily Dive, Apr 2026). Thirty years, built by buying operators. The Tesla analog is the same discipline applied to capabilities: acquire or build the adjacent layer when the platform's demand makes it accretive.

23Part VI · SAM / SOM

SAM and SOM To Model

SAM: the West Coast wedge (~$20-25B/yr) is right for Phases 1-2 and wrong for the 30-year story. By Phase 3 the constraint is code pathway coverage, not geography.

SOM: the existing ~$250M/yr by year 10 is a development SOM. A second SOM, units under management and infrastructure revenue, is where the valuation sits, and it does not yet exist.

Blocked until modeled
The layered SOM requires per-unit catalog economics and the phase ramp (inputs #2 and #3). Until then, this deck states the development SOM only, labeled as such.
24Part VII · The Bets

Five load-bearing bets

1Single-stair reform keeps spreading. Grounded: Seattle since 1977; WA statewide effective May 2027; OR four-story local opt-in live; CA stalled after a skeptical Fire Marshal report; 19 states + DC have introduced bills, 7 passed in 2025 (Pew), and Connecticut repealed in 2026. Without continued spread the chassis has no national market.
2Demand aggregation actually produces factory economics. Kombohus is the precedent; Katerra (~$2B raised, bankrupt 2021) is the counter-example. Our version is untested.
3Other developers will build with someone else's chassis. The biggest unproven claim in the plan.
4The operating layer produces a measurable premium. No Now City data exists.
5Operating data compounds into real advantage. Believable, unproven at n=1.
